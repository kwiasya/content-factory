CONTEXT (same for every research task in this series, 07–14)

I am a solo creator making videos for YouTube (long-form and Shorts), TikTok and
Instagram Reels, in whatever format fits the idea: talking head, voice-over on footage,
screencast, POV walks with music only, vlog, challenge. I am building an AI agent
(Claude Code: markdown "skills" plus Python) that writes video scripts, proposes hooks,
titles and structure, and critiques a draft script before I shoot or edit. This research
series collects the evidence-based rules that agent will follow. Platform priority:
YouTube first, TikTok second, Instagram third.

Research rules:
- Source hierarchy, best to worst: (1) statements, docs and published research from
  YouTube / Google, TikTok and Meta; (2) creators showing their own data (retention
  graphs, A/B thumbnail tests, analytics screenshots, internal documents);
  (3) analysts working from samples (Paddy Galloway, Colin and Samir, VidIQ, 1of10,
  Film Booth, Jon Youshaei and similar); (4) peer-reviewed research on attention,
  curiosity, suspense, emotion and narrative. Exclude marketing blogs, SEO listicles
  and paid-course content unless they quote a tier 1–4 source; then cite that source.
- Tag every rule or claim with its evidence level: DATA (a measurement over a sample,
  an A/B test or a retention graph is shown; one video's view count is not DATA),
  PRACTITIONER (a creator's or analyst's stated practice, no data shown) or THEORY
  (research or a model, not tested on video). Keep the three apart.
- Time horizon: for platforms, algorithms and creator case studies prefer 2024–2026
  and flag anything older as possibly outdated. For psychology, emotion and
  storytelling theory there is no date limit; classic sources are welcome.
- Examples must be concrete: a named video with a link and a timestamp (or a named
  creator and a named video), never "many creators do X". A timestamp taken from a
  transcript, a chapter list or a published breakdown is fine; say where it came from.
- Be explicit about what does NOT work, what is contested, and what is a myth that
  the sources themselves have retracted. Negative findings are valuable.
- Do not pad. Tables over prose. Cite a link for every claim.

RESEARCH TASK 08: hooks and the first 30 seconds (long-form) / first 3 seconds (short-form)

Goal: a hook system the agent can use to write 5–10 hook candidates for a video and to
critique them. I already have a working taxonomy of hook categories (below); validate it,
extend it, or tell me where it is wrong, with evidence.

My current hook categories (keep, rename, merge, split or add; justify each verdict):
- question: opens with a question the video answers
- curiosity: curiosity gap, withholds the key fact until later
- contrast: expectation against reality, before against after
- list: opens a count, N things, N places
- pov: puts the viewer inside a situation, "POV: you ..."
- visual_payoff: the first frame shows the reward the video promises
- transformation: turn X into Y
- diagnosis: the reason X fails is Y
- named_framework: a sharp promise first, then the name of the method
- numbered_series: episode N of X, with a tension line after the label
- reaction_promise: promises a reaction the viewer cannot skip
- bare_number (marked as a trap): a number with no mechanism in the same sentence
- fake_label (marked as a trap): a name with nothing behind it

Find and document:

1. The hook as a contract: title + thumbnail + first seconds as one promise; evidence
   that the first seconds must pay off the thumbnail, and how fast. (The cost of a
   broken promise over time is covered in task 12; here only what the opening must do.)
2. Hook categories in use by top creators and analysts (Hook Genius-style shape
   libraries, Film Booth, Jenny Hoyos, Paddy Galloway, Colin and Samir, VidIQ and
   others): for each category, when it works, when it fails, an example with a
   timestamp, and data if any. Map every category to my list above or propose a new
   one.
3. Spoken hook, visual hook, on-screen text hook: how they combine; what the first
   frame should contain; how many seconds until the promise is stated; what retention
   data says about each.
4. Hook formulas: promise + stakes + why now; context → conflict → question; "what,
   why, how"; "you'd think X, but Y"; others practitioners teach. For each: structure,
   an example, where it is overused.
5. Long-form specifics: cold open, in medias res, preview montage ("sizzle"), skipping
   the intro entirely, restating the premise at 30–60 s; evidence for and against
   preview montages in 2024–2026.
6. The writing process: how top writers iterate hooks (write 10, pick 1; table reads;
   testing hooks as Shorts or community posts), and prompt patterns people use to get
   LLMs to write hooks that are not generic.
7. Negative findings: hook shapes that were overused in 2025–2026 and now read as
   spam, platform statements about misleading openings, and what reviewers flag as a
   bad hook.

Output format:
- Table A: hook categories (category, definition, works for long / short, evidence
  level, example video + timestamp, verdict on my taxonomy: keep / rename / merge /
  add, with reason).
- Table B: hook formulas (formula, structure, example, overuse risk).
- Section C: rules for the first 30 s of long-form and the first 3 s of short-form,
  each tagged DATA / PRACTITIONER / THEORY. (Short-form detail is covered in task 11;
  here only what the hook itself must do.)
- Section D: a hook critique checklist: what a reviewer checks, in what order, with a
  pass / fail criterion for each item.
- Section E: anti-patterns and retracted advice.
