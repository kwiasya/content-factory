CONTEXT (same for every research task in this series, 07–14)

I am a solo creator making videos for YouTube (long-form and Shorts), TikTok and
Instagram Reels, in whatever format fits the idea: talking head, voice-over on footage,
screencast, POV walks with music only, vlog, challenge. I am building an AI agent
(Claude Code: markdown "skills" plus Python) that writes video scripts, proposes hooks,
titles and structure, and critiques a draft script before I shoot or edit. This research
series collects the evidence-based rules that agent will follow. Platform priority:
YouTube first, TikTok second, Instagram third.

Research rules:
- Source hierarchy, best to worst: (1) statements, docs and published research from
  YouTube / Google, TikTok and Meta; (2) creators showing their own data (retention
  graphs, A/B thumbnail tests, analytics screenshots, internal documents);
  (3) analysts working from samples (Paddy Galloway, Colin and Samir, VidIQ, 1of10,
  Film Booth, Jon Youshaei and similar); (4) peer-reviewed research on attention,
  curiosity, suspense, emotion and narrative. Exclude marketing blogs, SEO listicles
  and paid-course content unless they quote a tier 1–4 source; then cite that source.
- Tag every rule or claim with its evidence level: DATA (a measurement over a sample,
  an A/B test or a retention graph is shown; one video's view count is not DATA),
  PRACTITIONER (a creator's or analyst's stated practice, no data shown) or THEORY
  (research or a model, not tested on video). Keep the three apart.
- Time horizon: for platforms, algorithms and creator case studies prefer 2024–2026
  and flag anything older as possibly outdated. For psychology, emotion and
  storytelling theory there is no date limit; classic sources are welcome.
- Examples must be concrete: a named video with a link and a timestamp (or a named
  creator and a named video), never "many creators do X". A timestamp taken from a
  transcript, a chapter list or a published breakdown is fine; say where it came from.
- Be explicit about what does NOT work, what is contested, and what is a myth that
  the sources themselves have retracted. Negative findings are valuable.
- Do not pad. Tables over prose. Cite a link for every claim.

RESEARCH TASK 10: emotion and the psychology of attention, as it applies to video

Goal: the reasons behind the rules. For every mechanism that makes people keep watching,
feel something, and share, I want the research behind it, what it predicts for video,
and a practical rule, so the agent's rules have explanations and not just folklore.
No date limit for theory; platform and creator evidence should be 2024–2026.

Find and document:

1. Curiosity: information-gap theory (Loewenstein), curiosity as prediction error,
   studies on curiosity and attention in media; how a gap is opened and closed, how
   many can stay open, and how long a viewer tolerates an unclosed gap.
2. Suspense and anticipation: suspense research (Zillmann; Brewer and Lichtenstein's
   structural-affect theory), "anomalous suspense" (why a known outcome still grips);
   relevance to non-fiction; how the pacing of a reveal changes the effect.
3. Surprise, novelty and pattern interrupts: habituation, attention renewed by change
   (visual, audio, tempo), research on edit density, camera cuts and recall, and the
   downside of overstimulation.
4. Emotional arcs: Reagan et al.'s six story shapes, emotional contagion, valence and
   arousal; which emotions drive sharing (Berger and Milkman, high-arousal emotions)
   versus which drive watch time; where they conflict.
5. Parasocial relationships and trust: why a face and a voice retain; authenticity
   signals; what substitutes for them when there is no face on screen.
6. Humor and relief: comedic timing, relief after tension, callbacks; evidence.
7. Music and sound: tempo and arousal, silence as a tool, music changes as pattern
   interrupts; research on music and emotion in film and video.
8. Cognitive load: information per minute, working-memory limits, one idea per
   segment, dual coding (on-screen text plus speech: when it helps and when it
   competes), speech rate.
9. Ethics and limits: engagement versus manipulation, outrage and rage-bait, what the
   platforms say they penalize (YouTube's satisfaction surveys, "regretted" views),
   and where creators report long-term audience cost from short-term tricks.

Output format:
- Table A: mechanism × what it predicts for video × evidence (THEORY / DATA, with the
  key source) × practical rule for a script or an edit.
- Section B: emotion budgeting across a video: which emotion where (open, middle,
  payoff, end), for long-form and short-form, with the reasoning.
- Section C: contested or weak evidence: claims that are repeated by practitioners but
  not supported, or supported only outside video.
- Section D: a reading list, at most 10 items, ranked by usefulness for a script writer,
  one line each on why.
