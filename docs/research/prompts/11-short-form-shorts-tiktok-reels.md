CONTEXT (same for every research task in this series, 07–14)

I am a solo creator making videos for YouTube (long-form and Shorts), TikTok and
Instagram Reels, in whatever format fits the idea: talking head, voice-over on footage,
screencast, POV walks with music only, vlog, challenge. I am building an AI agent
(Claude Code: markdown "skills" plus Python) that writes video scripts, proposes hooks,
titles and structure, and critiques a draft script before I shoot or edit. This research
series collects the evidence-based rules that agent will follow. Platform priority:
YouTube first, TikTok second, Instagram third.

Research rules:
- Source hierarchy, best to worst: (1) statements, docs and published research from
  YouTube / Google, TikTok and Meta; (2) creators showing their own data (retention
  graphs, A/B thumbnail tests, analytics screenshots, internal documents);
  (3) analysts working from samples (Paddy Galloway, Colin and Samir, VidIQ, 1of10,
  Film Booth, Jon Youshaei and similar); (4) peer-reviewed research on attention,
  curiosity, suspense, emotion and narrative. Exclude marketing blogs, SEO listicles
  and paid-course content unless they quote a tier 1–4 source; then cite that source.
- Tag every rule or claim with its evidence level: DATA (a measurement over a sample,
  an A/B test or a retention graph is shown; one video's view count is not DATA),
  PRACTITIONER (a creator's or analyst's stated practice, no data shown) or THEORY
  (research or a model, not tested on video). Keep the three apart.
- Time horizon: for platforms, algorithms and creator case studies prefer 2024–2026
  and flag anything older as possibly outdated. For psychology, emotion and
  storytelling theory there is no date limit; classic sources are welcome.
- Examples must be concrete: a named video with a link and a timestamp (or a named
  creator and a named video), never "many creators do X". A timestamp taken from a
  transcript, a chapter list or a published breakdown is fine; say where it came from.
- Be explicit about what does NOT work, what is contested, and what is a myth that
  the sources themselves have retracted. Negative findings are valuable.
- Do not pad. Tables over prose. Cite a link for every claim.

RESEARCH TASK 11: short-form video on YouTube Shorts, TikTok and Instagram Reels

Goal: the rules for a 15–180 second vertical video on each of the three platforms, with
YouTube Shorts and TikTok at equal weight and Reels as the third column: what is the
same, what differs, and what the data says about retention in short-form.

My current hook categories (validate for short-form specifically; keep / rename /
merge / add with reason): question, curiosity, contrast, list, pov, visual_payoff,
transformation, diagnosis, named_framework, numbered_series, reaction_promise, and two
traps: bare_number, fake_label. My current visual layouts: broll_voiceover, pov_walk,
screencast, split, talking_head, text_on_footage.

Find and document:

1. Platform mechanics 2025–2026: how each platform ranks short videos (viewed versus
   swiped away, average percentage viewed, loops and replays, shares and sends,
   comments, follows from the video), with official statements. What changed recently:
   YouTube Shorts up to 3 minutes, Reels up to 3 minutes, TikTok's push for longer
   videos, Instagram's weighting of sends, Shorts in the long-form feed.
2. The first second: what the first frame, the first on-screen text and the first sound
   must do; the difference between a hook on a swipe feed and a hook on a click
   (long-form); data on swipe-away in the first 1–3 seconds.
3. Retention in short-form: the ideal curve shape, loop and replay design (end matches
   start, seamless loops), length versus completion rate, per-second retention
   thinking (Jenny Hoyos and others), what the analysts measured on samples.
4. Structure: beats for 15–30 s, 30–60 s and 60–180 s videos; where the payoff sits;
   how a 2-minute Short differs from a 30-second one; what structures survive the
   3-minute limit.
5. Text on screen and captions: amount, placement, current safe zones in pixels for
   1080×1920 on each platform (UI overlays: username, caption, buttons, progress bar;
   give the date of each source, the overlays change with app versions), font and size
   norms, whether burned-in captions help; data where it exists.
6. Audio: trending sounds, original audio, voice-over, music-only; how much each
   matters per platform in 2025–2026; licensing and what breaks when cross-posting.
7. Cross-posting: what works unchanged on all three, what must change (caption,
   hashtags, length, hook text, aspect ratio, safe zone); watermark penalties and the
   platforms' statements on reposted content.
8. Shorts as a funnel: do Shorts grow a long-form channel in 2025–2026; YouTube's
   statements and creator data; when they hurt.
9. Negative findings: short-form myths (posting time, hashtag counts, "the algorithm
   punishes X") that platform statements or data contradict.

Output format:
- Table A: three-column comparison (YouTube Shorts / TikTok / Instagram Reels) with at
  least these rows: ranking signals, maximum length, length that performs, safe zone,
  captions, audio, hashtags, posting cadence, loop behavior, cross-post penalties,
  what the analytics expose; each cell with a source.
- Table B: hook categories for short-form with verdicts on my taxonomy and an example
  video + timestamp for each.
- Section C: rules by segment: 0–1 s, 1–3 s, 3–15 s, the middle, the last 2 s; each
  tagged DATA / PRACTITIONER / THEORY.
- Section D: 15–20 example short videos across the three platforms (link, length,
  what it does in the first second, structure, why it performed).
- Section E: myths and contested claims.
