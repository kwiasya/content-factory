CONTEXT (same for every research task in this series, 07–14)

I am a solo creator making videos for YouTube (long-form and Shorts), TikTok and
Instagram Reels, in whatever format fits the idea: talking head, voice-over on footage,
screencast, POV walks with music only, vlog, challenge. I am building an AI agent
(Claude Code: markdown "skills" plus Python) that writes video scripts, proposes hooks,
titles and structure, and critiques a draft script before I shoot or edit. This research
series collects the evidence-based rules that agent will follow. Platform priority:
YouTube first, TikTok second, Instagram third.

Research rules:
- Source hierarchy, best to worst: (1) statements, docs and published research from
  YouTube / Google, TikTok and Meta; (2) creators showing their own data (retention
  graphs, A/B thumbnail tests, analytics screenshots, internal documents);
  (3) analysts working from samples (Paddy Galloway, Colin and Samir, VidIQ, 1of10,
  Film Booth, Jon Youshaei and similar); (4) peer-reviewed research on attention,
  curiosity, suspense, emotion and narrative. Exclude marketing blogs, SEO listicles
  and paid-course content unless they quote a tier 1–4 source; then cite that source.
- Tag every rule or claim with its evidence level: DATA (a measurement over a sample,
  an A/B test or a retention graph is shown; one video's view count is not DATA),
  PRACTITIONER (a creator's or analyst's stated practice, no data shown) or THEORY
  (research or a model, not tested on video). Keep the three apart.
- Time horizon: for platforms, algorithms and creator case studies prefer 2024–2026
  and flag anything older as possibly outdated. For psychology, emotion and
  storytelling theory there is no date limit; classic sources are welcome.
- Examples must be concrete: a named video with a link and a timestamp (or a named
  creator and a named video), never "many creators do X". A timestamp taken from a
  transcript, a chapter list or a published breakdown is fine; say where it came from.
- Be explicit about what does NOT work, what is contested, and what is a myth that
  the sources themselves have retracted. Negative findings are valuable.
- Do not pad. Tables over prose. Cite a link for every claim.

RESEARCH TASK 12: packaging (title, thumbnail) and idea validation before production

Goal: rules the agent can use to score an idea before anything is shot, to write and
rank titles, to brief a thumbnail, and to keep the packaging promise consistent with the
video so the hook can pay it off.

Note: I already have a pipeline that finds outlier videos in a niche (views against the
channel's median). I need the methodology for turning outliers into ideas and the
evidence on packaging, not a tool survey.

Find and document:

1. "Idea first": how top creators and analysts choose ideas (MrBeast's stated process,
   Paddy Galloway's idea × packaging × execution framing, outlier-based idea mining,
   audience-demand checks). How they score an idea before production; evidence that
   the idea dominates execution.
2. Titles: structures that perform (with CTR data where it exists), length, numbers,
   emotional and specific words, the title as complement to the thumbnail rather than
   a duplicate; YouTube's own statements on CTR, impressions and "impressions
   click-through rate" benchmarks; results from title A/B tests creators published.
3. Thumbnails: composition rules (face or no face, number of elements, contrast, text
   of 0–3 words, readability at phone size), style trends 2025–2026, what published A/B
   tests showed (YouTube's native Test & Compare results, TubeBuddy and
   thumbnailtest reports), and how to brief a thumbnail before shooting so the shot
   exists.
4. Packaging for short-form: whether title and cover matter on Shorts, TikTok and
   Reels; the first frame and the on-screen hook text as the thumbnail; what the
   platforms expose and what the data says.
5. Consistency: how the packaging promise sets up the retention curve; "clickbait
   debt" and its measured cost (satisfaction surveys, returning viewers); the line
   between a strong promise and a misleading one in platform policy.
6. Process: how many titles and thumbnails to draft, and how creators test them
   before and after publishing (polls, community posts, Shorts, Test & Compare). The
   statistics of the test itself (duration, sample size, confidence) are task 14; here
   only the drafting and testing practice.
7. Negative findings: packaging patterns that are saturated or penalized in 2025–2026
   (misleading-thumbnail policy, red arrows and circles, shocked faces), with the
   source that says so.

Output format:
- Table A: title patterns × evidence (CTR data or practitioner) × example title ×
  overuse risk.
- Table B: thumbnail rules × evidence × example (link to the video).
- Section C: an idea-scoring rubric derived from the sources: criteria, how to score
  each, what a pass looks like, with the source for each criterion.
- Section D: the drafting and testing practice: how many variants, which test goes
  where, in what order, with the source for each practice.
- Section E: anti-patterns and policy limits.
