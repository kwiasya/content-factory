CONTEXT (same for every research task in this series, 07–14)

I am a solo creator making videos for YouTube (long-form and Shorts), TikTok and
Instagram Reels, in whatever format fits the idea: talking head, voice-over on footage,
screencast, POV walks with music only, vlog, challenge. I am building an AI agent
(Claude Code: markdown "skills" plus Python) that writes video scripts, proposes hooks,
titles and structure, and critiques a draft script before I shoot or edit. This research
series collects the evidence-based rules that agent will follow. Platform priority:
YouTube first, TikTok second, Instagram third.

Research rules:
- Source hierarchy, best to worst: (1) statements, docs and published research from
  YouTube / Google, TikTok and Meta; (2) creators showing their own data (retention
  graphs, A/B thumbnail tests, analytics screenshots, internal documents);
  (3) analysts working from samples (Paddy Galloway, Colin and Samir, VidIQ, 1of10,
  Film Booth, Jon Youshaei and similar); (4) peer-reviewed research on attention,
  curiosity, suspense, emotion and narrative. Exclude marketing blogs, SEO listicles
  and paid-course content unless they quote a tier 1–4 source; then cite that source.
- Tag every rule or claim with its evidence level: DATA (a measurement over a sample,
  an A/B test or a retention graph is shown; one video's view count is not DATA),
  PRACTITIONER (a creator's or analyst's stated practice, no data shown) or THEORY
  (research or a model, not tested on video). Keep the three apart.
- Time horizon: for platforms, algorithms and creator case studies prefer 2024–2026
  and flag anything older as possibly outdated. For psychology, emotion and
  storytelling theory there is no date limit; classic sources are welcome.
- Examples must be concrete: a named video with a link and a timestamp (or a named
  creator and a named video), never "many creators do X". A timestamp taken from a
  transcript, a chapter list or a published breakdown is fine; say where it came from.
- Be explicit about what does NOT work, what is contested, and what is a myth that
  the sources themselves have retracted. Negative findings are valuable.
- Do not pad. Tables over prose. Cite a link for every claim.

RESEARCH TASK 07: audience retention in long-form YouTube video (roughly 8–30 minutes)

Goal: understand the retention curve well enough to write rules an agent can apply when
it scripts a long video and when it critiques a draft. I want the anatomy of the curve,
what moves each part of it, and how to diagnose a curve after publishing.

Find and document:

1. Anatomy of a retention curve: the typical shape, the drop in the first 30 seconds,
   the slope of the middle, the end drop. Benchmarks by video length and genre (essay,
   vlog, tutorial, challenge, documentary) for: retention at 30 s, average view
   duration, average percentage viewed. What YouTube itself publishes about "intro"
   and "key moments" in Studio, and what analysts measured on samples.
2. The first 30 seconds: YouTube's own guidance; creator data on cold opens versus
   branded intros; how much of the early drop is wrong viewers (packaging mismatch)
   versus a slow opening, and how to tell the two apart.
3. Keeping the middle: open loops, teasers, "but first", chapter promises, stakes
   escalation, pattern interrupts, b-roll change rate, music changes, re-hooks at
   chapter boundaries. For each technique: evidence of its effect on the curve, an
   example video with a timestamp, and when it backfires.
4. Pacing and editing tempo: cuts per minute, dead air, speech rate, jump cuts, visual
   change rate. Which numbers are backed by data and which are folklore. Genre
   differences, and the 2024–2026 shift toward slower, longer videos (what creators
   and YouTube said about it).
5. Reading the curve for diagnosis: spikes (rewatched moments), dips, a cliff versus a
   slope, relative versus absolute retention, returning versus new viewers. How creators
   iterate on it (checkpoint ideas like "minute 1, minute 3, minute 6"), with sources.
6. Length: how length interacts with retention, watch time and reach; the "longer is
   better" debate with data; YouTube's 2025–2026 position on watch time versus viewer
   satisfaction (surveys, "valued watch time", "regret" signals).
7. Endings: ending without a cliff, end screens, hand-off to the next video, what to
   never say in the last 30 seconds; evidence.
8. Negative findings: tricks that stopped working or got penalized (clickbait intros,
   fake open loops, artificial stretching), and claims the sources themselves retracted.

Exclude: live streams, podcasts longer than 60 minutes, kids content.

Output format:
- Table A: retention benchmarks (length × genre × metric × value × source × DATA /
  PRACTITIONER).
- Section B: timeline of a long video (0–5 s, 5–30 s, 30 s–2 min, the middle, the last
  10 %) with the rules for each segment, each rule tagged with its evidence level.
- Table C: techniques × effect on the curve × evidence × example video + timestamp ×
  when it backfires.
- Section D: diagnosis guide: curve shape → likely cause → what to change next time.
- Section E: myths and contested claims, each with who claims what.
