# B. Analytics Diagnosis Playbook

| **Symptom**                                     | **Likely Cause**                                                 | **Next Experiment**                                                                                                |
|-----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Low CTR (low views per impression)**        | Thumbnail/title not compelling or mismatched to content; or traffic source is “cold” (suggested vs. subscriptions). | Test new thumbnail/title (A/B test if possible). If Live, reupload thumbnail or use “Test & Compare” when available. Also target a more relevant audience or keywords. |
| **High CTR but low retention**                | Clickbait-style packaging: high promise but content doesn’t deliver. Your hook misaligned. | Examine intro: was topic clearly delivered? Re-shoot tighter intro or move value earlier. For next video, preview value in first seconds. |
| **Low overall retention (steep drop at start)** | Weak or slow hook; viewers’ expectations not met immediately. Or title misled them.    | Rework opening: shorten intro, highlight value. For existing video, try moving key footage earlier. Next video: test a stronger hook. |
| **Retention “cliff” ~30s**                    | Introductions too long/boring; viewers lose interest by 30s.                        | Cut intro fluff. Experiment: start with key message/action. Use A/B: one version with jump-cut hook vs original. |
| **Gradual retention decline (no sharp drops)**| Normal; viewers slowly drop off as video ends. Content pacing is OK.               | Maintain format. If baseline is good, experiment with small changes (e.g. better mid-video engagement). |
| **Retention spike mid-video**                 | Likely viewers replaying a part (e.g. punchline or confusing bit) or shared link to that timestamp. | Rewatch that moment: if it’s a highlight (joke, reveal), plan more content like it. If confusion (replay), clarify edit. |
| **Sharp dip after a spike**                   | Could be a single viewer rewound or a popular moment causing transient spike. | Don’t overreact: spikes are noisy. Verify with larger sample (use advanced mode by traffic source/device). If pattern repeats, probe content. |
| **Low impressions (limited reach)**          | Low initial engagement or niche topic keeps algorithm from pushing video; or unoptimized metadata. | Improve SEO: add relevant keywords/tags/descriptions. Share video externally to boost early views. Consider broadening topic or promoting via Shorts/communities. |
| **Views high but impressions stagnate**      | Saturated core audience; no growth from recommendations.              | Engage subscribers (CTA to share). Create content series to build returning viewers. Experiment with new topics or cross-promotion to refresh audience. |
| **Low returning viewers (audience growth)**  | Viewers aren’t “hooked” into series; content may feel one-off.      | Try consistent format/theme or upload schedule. Experiment with part-series or on-brand content to build habit. |
| **Irregular traffic sources (e.g. mostly Social/External)** | Video got shared off-platform; or SEO poor (not in Search).         | If high external: lean into those channels (promote more there). If almost no Search: add clear titles/metadata, ask community to search, test SEO-friendly topics. |

*Notes:* As YT expert Alan Spicer notes, **CTR and retention must be interpreted together** – a video with high CTR but fast dropoff is a “clickbait trap”. A cliff in the first 30s almost always signals a hook problem. Spikes can mean rewatches or confusion. Always validate with multiple videos or segments (Advanced Mode segmentation) to avoid overinterpreting small-sample quirks.

# D. Experiment Protocol for Small Channels

- **Use Platform A/B tools if available:** YouTube’s upcoming *Test & Compare* (in Beta) will let you run A/B tests on up to 3 thumbnails or title+thumbnail combos, measuring watch time. When released, use it (it typically runs up to 2 weeks or until you stop it). TikTok currently has no built-in A/B feature; one can use sequential tests (e.g. post Video A vs Video B with single variable change) but control for time-of-posting and topic.
- **Minimum sample:** For meaningful results, aim for *at least* several thousand impressions per variant. With only ~1–2K views, random noise dominates. (Statistically, e.g. a jump from 5% to 6% CTR on 5K impressions yields only ~p=0.07, i.e. <93% confidence.) Expect only **~80–90%** confidence on large changes; small differences (<1–2% CTR or a few seconds of watch time) won’t be significant.
- **Wait time:** Don’t judge too soon. On YouTube, performance continues evolving past 24–48h. Prefer waiting 7–14 days (or until growth plateaus) before concluding. TikTok settles faster but allow at least 48h to a week.
- **Comparing across videos:** If testing by uploading different videos (A vs B), control for day/time and content. Use YouTube’s *Advanced Mode* to filter by “first 48h” or “first week” for each video, ensuring equal time windows. Normalize for traffic source mix if possible (e.g. compare only “Suggested” traffic).
- **Logging hypotheses:** Keep a simple log (spreadsheet or Notion) with columns: date, video ID, tested element (thumbnail/title/hooks), hypothesis, result metric (CTR/Retention), verdict. E.g. *“Test: Red vs blue thumbnail. Hypothesis: blue yields +10% CTR. Result: CTR(A)=4.5%, CTR(B)=5.2% on ~3K imp. (P≈0.18).”* Some small channels share their processes (see creator Casey Neistat or Colin & Samir experiments), but we could not find a citable public template. Use consistent naming and track at least CTR and 50% retention to compare.
- **Decision threshold:** Given low data, avoid “statistical significance” mania. Look for *meaningful* changes (e.g. CTR jump of 2–3 pp or retention up by 10s) plus consistent trend over multiple videos. Realistically, treat 75–90% confidence as actionable. For very small channels, consider repeating experiments multiple times to build evidence.

# E. Limitations & Misreadings

- **Vanity metrics:** Raw views or likes alone are poor guides. Especially for Shorts, views can be inflated by looping (YouTube now counts a view on any loop). TikTok “views” similarly count on first frame. Never judge a video solely by view count. Focus on *quality* engagement (watch time, percent watched, comments) over raw reach.
- **Retention chart traps:** A retention “spike” is not automatically a “good moment”. It could mean viewers replayed a scene (good) **or** were confused and re-listened (not good). Likewise, a “dip” could simply follow a spike. Always verify by watching the footage around that timestamp before editing it out. As ArcFlo warns, the graph shows **behavior**, not intent.
- **Across-video comparisons:** Be careful comparing metrics across videos of different length, format, or topic. A 50% average view on a 10-min vlog vs a 50% on a 2-min short are not equivalent. Normalize for duration (YouTube’s “Average % viewed” helps). Also compare similar types: e.g. news videos to other news videos, not to comedy skits.
- **Demographics not available at micro-level:** YouTube/TikTok do not reveal viewers’ age/gender per video (only aggregated). Instagram used to show age of Reels watchers, but Graph API doesn’t expose it for Reels. So you **cannot** know exactly who is dropping off (only approximate via “Returning vs New” or overall channel).
- **Unknown factors:** You cannot measure **why** someone left or liked your video. Emotions, attention (eyes-on-screen), multitasking: none are captured. Surveys exist (YouTube’s optional thumbs-up/down after video) but usage is low and not easily extracted via API.
- **Misattribute cause:** Algorithm changes and seasonality are *outside* your control and may affect metrics. For example, YouTube may cap impressions for older videos or push Shorts more than long-form unpredictably. These platform-level changes are unmeasurable from your analytics.
- **TikTok/TikTok API limits:** TikTok’s official **watch-time** and **completion-rate** metrics are only in the app or Creator Studio. No public API returns these. If a tool claims to give avg watch time for arbitrary TikTok videos, it’s reverse-engineering or scraping – treat it skeptically.
- **Instagram Reels retention:** The new Reels retention/skip metrics announced are only in the mobile Analytics UI and *not* available via the Graph API. External tools cannot fetch the “skip rate” for Reels.
- **Small-sample noise:** On small channels, individual viewers (or even the algorithm’s randomness) can swing CTR and retention. Don’t chase minor fluctuations. Only systematic patterns (consistent across multiple posts) are reliable.

*References:* Our diagnosis rules come from YouTube creator experts, and known pitfalls from creator experience. The TikTok API limitations are documented by TikLiveAPI. Negative findings (like the *lack* of watch-time in the public API) are explicitly called out.
