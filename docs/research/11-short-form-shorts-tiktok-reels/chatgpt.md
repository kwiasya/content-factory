# Short-form Video Research for YouTube Shorts, TikTok and Instagram Reels

## Executive findings and evidence key

The central finding is that **short-form hooks are not primarily sentences; they are a synchronized first-frame system**. On a swipe feed, the viewer has not first committed by clicking a title and thumbnail. The opening image, on-screen text, and—when sound is on—the first audible beat must jointly answer **“What is this?”**, **“Why should I care?”**, and **“What unresolved thing will I get by staying?”** almost immediately. Jenny Hoyos describes Shorts as giving the creator roughly one second to hook, and YouTube itself featured that advice in a 2025 discussion with Shorts product lead Todd Sherman.

**Evidence labels.** I use your three requested labels strictly as far as possible. `DATA` means a measurement, analytics example, sample analysis, or experiment is actually shown. `PRACTITIONER` means a creator, analyst, or platform operator states a practice/mechanic without publishing a controlled measurement. `THEORY` means a psychological/story model not directly tested on short-form video. Because your taxonomy has no separate `PLATFORM` label, an official platform claim about an unpublished ranking system is marked **PRACTITIONER | T1 official** rather than being upgraded to DATA.

The other high-confidence conclusions are:

**[PRACTITIONER | T1] There is no public formula saying “completion = X%, shares = Y%, comments = Z%” on any of the three platforms.** YouTube exposes Shorts-specific measures including “stayed to watch,” engaged views, average view duration and average percentage viewed; TikTok says recommendations depend on user interactions and currently describes the For You feed as learning from what people watch, like and share; Instagram head Adam Mosseri’s 2025 explanation names **watch time, likes and sends** as its main broad ranking factors, with likes relatively more important for connected reach and sends for unconnected reach.

**[DATA | T1] Do not equate a public “view” with a high-quality view.** YouTube’s current Shorts analytics calculates AVD and APV from **engaged views among viewers who stayed to watch**, not from every public view; Instagram changed to views as its primary cross-format metric and explicitly notes that repeat views by one person can count again. The useful quality layer is therefore retention/watch behavior plus satisfaction actions—not raw starts alone.

**[PRACTITIONER | T1] TikTok has a stronger institutional incentive for longer short-form than the other two.** Its Creator Rewards Program specifically rewards qualifying, high-quality original videos longer than one minute. That is evidence of TikTok wanting creators to make longer material, **not evidence that a 61-second video receives a ranking bonus merely for crossing one minute**.

**[PRACTITIONER | T1] Instagram’s distinctive signal is private sharing.** Mosseri said in 2024 that sends per reach correlated with overall reach more strongly, in his experience, than other engagement rates; his subsequent 2025 framing broadened this to watch time, likes and sends as the three most important overall signals. Those statements are best read as an evolution/clarification, not as an exact published weighting formula.

**[DATA/PRACTITIONER | T2] “Every second counts” becomes literally useful in Shorts analytics.** In Jenny Hoyos’s worked examples, a single weak ending second produced a dramatic last-second retention cliff; she describes editing to the frame where the promise is fulfilled and stopping. Her broader targets—such as aiming around the 90% retention range and preferring roughly thirty-something-second stories—are **her operating benchmarks**, not universal YouTube thresholds. Her interview is from 2023, so treat the exact benchmarks as possibly outdated while retaining the editing principle.

**[DATA | T3, older] A very large third-party Shorts sample found a relationship between “viewed versus swiped away” and performance, but no magic threshold.** Antoine Lacombled’s analysis of billions of Shorts views reported that Shorts below roughly 60% viewed-versus-swiped tended to perform poorly and that stronger ranges were commonly around 70–90%, while also showing that a high VVSA was insufficient by itself. This is useful as a diagnostic prior, **not a platform rule**, and the study predates several major 2024–2026 Shorts changes.

**[NEGATIVE DATA FINDING] I did not find credible Tier 1–3 evidence for a universal “retain X% at exactly one second / three seconds or the video dies” benchmark across Shorts, TikTok and Reels.** The platforms expose overall/retention metrics and creators show individual graphs, but the widely repeated three-second thresholds are usually unsourced, ad-specific, or based on individual accounts. The agent should therefore optimize the first three seconds aggressively without encoding a universal survival percentage.

**[PRACTITIONER | T3] For 1080×1920, there is also no durable official organic-video pixel-safe-zone standard shared by all devices.** UI changes by device, caption length, account state and platform experiments. Independent overlays put TikTok’s heavy obstruction around the bottom ~484 px and right ~140 px, while Instagram estimates vary roughly from 108–220 px at top and 320–430 px at bottom. Jon Loomer’s updated 2025 conclusion is that a single conservative cross-platform template is safer because the interfaces increasingly overlap. A robust master safe rectangle is approximately **x = 60–940, y = 220–1490** on a 1080×1920 canvas.

For your **visual-layout taxonomy**, keep `broll_voiceover`, `pov_walk`, `screencast`, `split`, `talking_head`, and `text_on_footage`. **[PRACTITIONER]** I would rename `split` to `split_context` because split-screen is useful only when the second pane supplies simultaneous proof, reaction or context; add `hands_demo`/`object_demo` for demonstrations that begin mid-action; and add `result_first_montage` for before/after, food, making, repair and visual-reveal formats. These are layout labels and should remain independent of the hook taxonomy.

The agent should therefore model hooks as composable features rather than exactly one category:

`hook = mechanism + framing + proof + optional packaging`

For example, “I’m going to prove my grandma wrong by finding the best Christmas gift for $5” is simultaneously `constraint_challenge + reaction_or_test + stakes_open_loop`; `$5` is useful because it creates a constraint, whereas the same video introduced as “5 Christmas tips” would be a `bare_number` failure. Hoyos herself describes the example as an A-story plus an emotional B-story. **[PRACTITIONER | T2]**

## Section C — Rules by segment

| Segment | Agent rule | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **0–1 s** | **First frame:** show the subject/action/result/anomaly immediately. No fade from black, logo sting, scenic establishing shot, “hey guys,” or preparatory camera movement unless that image itself is the content. **First text:** one immediately decodable premise, preferably one line; it should add stakes/context the image cannot supply rather than transcribe the first spoken sentence. **First sound:** if sound matters, start with the meaningful word/impact/action rather than music lead-in. But the image must remain interpretable muted. | **[PRACTITIONER \| T1/T2]** YouTube’s 2025 Shorts discussion explicitly emphasizes roughly one second to hook; Hoyos says good Shorts are highly visual and should be understandable without sound. |
| **1–3 s** | **Prove the premise.** Show that the thing promised at 0:00 is actually happening: reveal the constraint, object, screen result, opponent, price, destination or problem. The first three seconds should reduce **confusion** while preserving **outcome uncertainty**. Never spend this interval on credentials/backstory unless the credential itself is the surprise. | **[PRACTITIONER]** This follows Hoyos’s “hook → intrigue → satisfy” approach and her insistence that every second has to earn the next. **[NEGATIVE DATA FINDING]** No defensible universal organic-feed “3-second survival threshold” was found. |
| **3–15 s** | Deliver the **first real reward**, not merely more setup. A 15–30 s video should usually be in the action by second 3 and have yielded new information/comedy/progress well before second 15. A 60–180 s video can still be setting up its macro-story, but needs a micro-payoff: first failed attempt, discovery, surprising fact, price reveal, obstacle or mini-result. | **[PRACTITIONER \| T2]** Hoyos’s short-form structure is built around continuing visual/narrative progression rather than delaying the promised action, and she advises ending immediately after the promise is delivered. |

Use periodic **reason-to-continue resets**, but they should be real developments, not “wait until the end.” | **[PRACTITIONER]** TikTok’s >1-minute Creator Rewards push confirms that one-to-three-minute material is strategically native, but supplies no evidence that padded length ranks better. **[THEORY]** Narrative progression works because uncertainty can be renewed by changes in goals, obstacles and predictions rather than by withholding one answer indefinitely. |
| **Last 2 s** | **Pay first, CTA second.** The promised transformation/result/reaction should happen before “follow for part two,” “subscribe,” or an external ask. **Retention curve target.** **[PRACTITIONER]** The useful ideal is not “a perfectly flat line.” Expect the largest unavoidable decision loss near the beginning, then aim for a **shallow decline with no unexplained cliffs**; spikes/rebounds can indicate replay or scrubbing. A cliff should map to an identifiable event: explanation slowed, outcome was already obvious, shot became redundant, CTA began, or viewer realized the promise was misleading. YouTube’s AVD/APV definitions make this diagnosis more meaningful than raw view count.

**Length versus completion.** **[PRACTITIONER]** Completion percentage is mechanically harder to maximize as duration rises, so comparing a 22-second Short with a 145-second Short on completion rate alone is analytically wrong. For short videos, APV/rewatch can dominate the diagnosis; for longer shorts, absolute watch time and sustained retention become more informative. Hoyos explicitly discusses the relationship between length, retention and rewatchability rather than treating one percentage as universal.

**[PRACTITIONER | T2, older] Hoyos’s rule of thumb is particularly useful for the agent:** each second has to make the next second feel necessary, and the video should end essentially when it has done what it said it would do. That is a better generative instruction than “make lots of cuts.”

**[NEGATIVE FINDING] “Pattern interrupt every two seconds” is not supported as a general law.** A cut, zoom or caption animation that adds no new information may increase sensory change without increasing curiosity. A single-take physical-comedy clip, POV walk, transformation or screen recording can retain strongly when **state changes continuously inside the shot**. The agent should ask “what changed for the viewer?” rather than “has two seconds passed?”

**Burned-caption rule:** **[PRACTITIONER]** for your 1080×1920 master, start around 52–76 px for ordinary spoken captions, one or two lines, and keep semantic text in the conservative safe rectangle. More important than exact font size is **instant legibility at phone distance**. Do not animate every word merely because short-form caption presets allow it; emphasize nouns, numbers, contradictions and state changes.

**Loop rule:** **[PRACTITIONER]** a seamless loop is valuable only when **the second viewing gives additional value**: reveal→recontextualization, motion whose end matches its beginning, or a sentence whose final words grammatically feed the opening. A loop that simply conceals the ending violates satisfaction. No platform has published evidence for a special “seamless loop ranking coefficient.”

**Two-minute versus thirty-second rule:** **[PRACTITIONER]** a two-minute Short cannot simply be a thirty-second Short spoken four times slower. It needs **multiple paid-off questions**. The macro-question can last two minutes, but every 10–30 seconds the viewer should learn something consequential enough that staying felt worthwhile. TikTok’s support for >1-minute original creator content makes this structure commercially relevant, while YouTube and Instagram’s three-minute limits now allow the same class of story.

**Shorts as a long-form funnel.** **[PRACTITIONER]** Shorts can grow a YouTube channel, but “Short subscriber” and “long-form viewer” are not interchangeable. The safest funnel is **topic congruence**: make the Short a complete, satisfying piece that attracts the same person the long video is for; then use the long video as the obvious next depth level. Hoyos treats Shorts-to-long-form conversion as a separate creative problem, not an automatic consequence of reach.

**[PRACTITIONER] When Shorts hurt the funnel, the likely mechanism is audience mismatch, not a secret channel punishment.** A broad meme/celebrity/novelty Short can gain large numbers of people with no interest in your twenty-minute travel essay, AI tutorial or vlog. The problematic metric is therefore **qualified downstream viewers**, not “subscribers gained.” An agent should penalize a Shorts idea whose appeal is substantially broader/different from the long-form video it is supposed to support.

## Section E — Myths, contested claims and agent rules

| Claim | Verdict | What the agent should encode |
|---|---|---|
| **“Completion rate is the Shorts/TikTok/Reels algorithm.”** | **MYTH. [PRACTITIONER/DATA]** All three systems have multi-signal recommendation logic. YouTube exposes AVD/APV and stayed-to-watch; TikTok describes multiple interaction/content/user signals; Instagram names watch time, likes and sends. | `Never optimize completion in isolation. Evaluate opening choice, watch duration/APV, replay, satisfaction/share behavior and follows/conversion together.` |
| **“A Short needs 70% viewed-versus-swiped or it dies.”** | **CONTESTED. [DATA \| T3, older]** A multi-billion-view third-party analysis found roughly that region useful diagnostically, but also showed high VVSA did not guarantee performance. YouTube has never published it as a threshold. | `Use VVSA as relative diagnostic within creator/account/length cohorts, never as hard eligibility gate.` |
| **“You have exactly three seconds to hook.”** | **OVERSTATED. [PRACTITIONER]** Hoyos argues Shorts are even harsher—roughly one second—but there is no universal published organic-feed three-second survival threshold across all three platforms. | `Treat frame 0–1 as hook establishment; seconds 1–3 as proof/clarification. Do not hard-code a retention percentage.` |
| **“Every two seconds needs a pattern interrupt.”** | **MYTH / unsupported. [PRACTITIONER]** Successful physical comedy, POV, food/process and illusion videos can hold a shot while the **state inside it changes**. | `Require information/progress change, not edit change.` |

Instagram explicitly counts repeated viewing in views; YouTube measures engaged-view watch behavior. | `Use loops for voluntary rewatch/recontextualization; reject loops that withhold the payoff.` |

[PRACTITIONER \| T1]** TikTok explicitly pays qualifying creators for original videos >1 minute; Shorts and Reels now support three-minute narratives. [PRACTITIONER \| T1]** The >1-minute requirement proves a monetization incentive, not an organic recommendation bonus. | `Never translate monetization eligibility directly into ranking weight.` |

[PRACTITIONER]** Mosseri strongly emphasized sends per reach in 2024; his 2025 explanation lists **watch time + likes + sends**, with sends especially important for unconnected reach. | `For Reels, optimize both hold and send-worthiness; ask “who would the viewer DM this to?”` |
| **“Likes are useless on Reels.”** | **FALSE. [PRACTITIONER]** Mosseri’s 2025 explanation says likes remain one of the top three and matter relatively more for connected reach. | `Distinguish follower/connected performance from non-follower distribution.` |

Jon Loomer explicitly recommends a combined cross-platform safe zone. | `Use conservative master x≈60–940, y≈220–1490; test platform preview before publish.` |

[PRACTITIONER]** Instagram explicitly tells creators not to use watermarked content for recommendations; equivalent blanket YouTube/TikTok ranking penalties are not publicly established. [PRACTITIONER]** Timing can change which followers are initially available, but the published ranking descriptions emphasize viewer response, not a privileged clock window. | `Use audience-online data as scheduling hygiene, not as creative-ranking logic.` |
| **“You must post every day.”** | **MYTH. [PRACTITIONER]** No credible official rule says missing a day creates an algorithmic penalty. High frequency primarily gives more attempts/data and maintains audience habit. | `Optimize sustainable experiment count × idea quality, not streak length.` |

[PRACTITIONER \| T2/T1-hosted]** Hoyos says the overwhelming majority of her Shorts discovery came from the Shorts feed, making thumbnails much less important there, but thumbnails still matter for channel presentation and other YouTube surfaces. | `Low priority for swipe-feed acquisition; retain coherent cover for channel/search/home branding.` |
| **“A numbered series is a hook.”** | **FALSE TAXONOMICALLY. [PRACTITIONER]** It tells an existing viewer where they are in a sequence; it does not tell a cold viewer why this episode matters. | `Store series_number as packaging metadata; require independent hook mechanism.` |
| **“POV: is itself a hook.”** | **FALSE. [PRACTITIONER]** It works only when it instantly installs a recognizable situation. | `Reject POV label if removing “POV:” leaves curiosity unchanged.` |
| **“A list is a hook because it has a number.”** | **FALSE. [PRACTITIONER]** The useful mechanism is bounded progress plus consequence/specificity, not the numeral itself. | `Reject bare_number unless each item set has clear problem, audience or stakes.` |
| **“Shorts automatically grow long-form.”** | **FALSE. [PRACTITIONER]** Shorts can be a strong same-platform discovery funnel, but Hoyos treats conversion as a distinct problem. | **“Shorts algorithmically damage a long-form channel.”** | **NOT ESTABLISHED. [PRACTITIONER]** The more defensible risk is **audience mismatch**, not a secret punitive switch. YouTube’s recommendation environment is personalized, while different formats can attract different cohorts. | `Warn about audience dilution; do not state that posting Shorts creates a channel penalty.` |
| **“The first frame should tease without explaining anything.”** | **BAD RULE. [PRACTITIONER]** Mystery and confusion are different. Hoyos’s work combines intrigue with very fast visual comprehension. | `Maximize outcome uncertainty while minimizing premise uncertainty.` |

The most useful compact rule set for the scripting agent is therefore:

**[PRACTITIONER]** In a swipe feed, **frame zero sells the premise; seconds one to three prove it; the body repeatedly changes the viewer’s information/state; the ending pays the exact promise and stops.** This is consistent with Hoyos’s per-second editing practice and with the platforms’ emphasis on actual watch behavior rather than metadata tricks.

**[PRACTITIONER]** Generate 15–30 s scripts around **one question and one payoff**; 30–60 s scripts around **one macro-question plus two to four meaningful turns**; 60–180 s scripts around **one macro-question plus recurring micro-payoffs**, with no extra runtime merely because three minutes are available. TikTok’s longer-video monetization, YouTube’s three-minute Shorts expansion and Instagram’s three-minute Reels expansion make the longer form legitimate, but none proves that duration itself earns distribution.

**[PRACTITIONER]** For cross-platform production, script the **visual hook and narrative identically first**, produce one clean 1080×1920 master, then localize packaging. Instagram gets the strongest “would someone send this?” check; TikTok gets the strongest search/trend/native-culture check and can justify >1-minute storytelling; YouTube Shorts gets the strongest **same-channel long-form-fit** check. Instagram’s published emphasis on sends, TikTok’s push toward original >1-minute creator work, and YouTube’s integrated Shorts/long-form ecosystem support those different priorities.

**[DATA/PRACTITIONER]** Finally, the agent should never pronounce a script “good” from predicted retention percentages alone. YouTube itself separates engaged-view quality metrics from simple view starts, the available large-sample Shorts work shows strong swipe metrics are not sufficient, and Instagram explicitly uses several distinct engagement/watch signals. The useful critique is causal and local: **where can the viewer already predict the payoff, where is the premise still unclear, where does nothing change, and where does the video continue after satisfaction?**
