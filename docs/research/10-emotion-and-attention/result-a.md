## Table A · Mechanisms

### A1. Curiosity (item 1)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1.1 Information gap.** Curiosity is deprivation that arises when attention falls on a gap in knowledge. It is intense, transient, and "tends to disappoint when satisfied". | A gap works only if the viewer notices it. A payoff that merely equals the promise feels flat. | THEORY. [Loewenstein 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) | Say the question out loud (VO or text). Make the payoff bigger or stranger than the promise. |
| **1.2 Inverted U on confidence.** Curiosity peaks when the viewer half-knows the answer (fMRI, n = 19, 40 trivia questions). | Questions about wholly unfamiliar or wholly obvious things pull little. | THEORY. [Kang et al. 2009](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf) | Open gaps on topics the viewer partly knows ("you've seen X; here's what you missed"). |
| **1.3 The half-known rule is conditional.** When a stimulus is unlikely to recur, novelty (maximal uncertainty) wins over intermediate uncertainty. | Pure novelty, such as an unseen place, can pull on its own. | THEORY. [Dubey & Griffiths 2019/2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000175) | On a POV walk, show the never-seen view early; novelty substitutes for a stated question. |
| **1.4 Curiosity is a reward signal.** At high curiosity people spent scarce tokens or waited 5–25 s for the answer; ratings tracked caudate activity. | "Waiting" is the lab version of not swiping. | THEORY. [Kang et al. 2009](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf) | Treat each open question as something the viewer is paying for in seconds; pay it back. |
| **1.5 Curiosity + prediction error.** Answers that contradicted the participant's guess were remembered best (d = 1.11, 12 of 16 returned). Learning tracks information prediction error more than information value. Generating a prediction raises curiosity. | Ask, let the viewer guess, then break the guess. | THEORY. [Kang et al. 2009](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf) Exp. 2; [Marvin & Shohamy 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000140); [Brod & Breitwieser 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6803639/) | "Guess before I show you" beats; the reveal should be different from the obvious guess. Samples are very small; see C. |
| **1.6 The anticipation window.** During high-curiosity waits (14 s), memory improved even for unrelated faces; at 1 day only for confidently recognised faces. Tracked SN/VTA and hippocampus. Authors: "small, but significant". | Material shown while a question is open is encoded better. | THEORY. [Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/). Counter: curiosity gives "only a small boost in learning" ([Wade & Kidd 2019](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-019-01598-6)) | Put exposition *inside* an open loop, not before it opens. Do not expect a large memory gain. |
| **1.7 Teasing effect.** Creating then resolving uncertainty is enjoyed more than getting the same information at once, yet people *choose* all-at-once (7 studies, incl. an ad). | A writer's "just say it" instinct is unreliable. | THEORY (incl. ad stimuli). [Ruan, Hsee & Lu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.15.0346) | Use short tease → resolve beats, even in a 30-s video. |
| **1.8 Curiosity while watching.** In a gradually revealed drawing task (>2,000 people, 19–76), higher curiosity made people keep watching instead of skipping to the spoiler. Curiosity peaks twice: at maximum uncertainty and just before the reveal. Its tone may shift toward frustration near the reveal (hedged in the source). | The closest lab analogue to "swipe or stay". A stalled final reveal risks exits. | DATA (lab, watching task). [Hsiung, Poh, Huettel & Adcock 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2301974120); authors' [SciAm summary](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/no-spoilers-please-why-curiosity-makes-us-patient/) (2024-04-09) | Pay off soon after the pre-reveal peak. Do not add a "but first" at the last step. |
| **1.9 Forward reference in packaging.** In 24,333 Upworthy pairs, third-person (β = .22) and first-person (β = .24) singular pronouns, negative-emotion words (β = .18) and length predicted CTR; accuracy 54.4% vs 50% chance ("still low"). In a second analysis, forward-reference language ("this", "he") raised CTR, while announcing an explanation ("answers a question") lowered it; prior literature predicted the direction badly (5 effects as predicted, 4 opposite). | Withholding a referent helps a little. Saying "I'll explain" does not. | THEORY (text, measured; 2013–15 data, old). [Gligorić et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281682); [Banerjee & Urminsky 2025](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2021.0018) (only the 2021 [draft](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf) was read). Notes tag both DATA-field | Titles and first lines may hold back *who/what* ("he", "this"). Do not promise explanation as the hook. Effects are modest. |
| **1.10 Concrete anchor.** Headlines can carry too much or too little information; clickbait is a continuum. | A gap with no concrete anchor underperforms a specific one. | THEORY (talk abstract). [Le Quere 2024](https://talks.cam.ac.uk/talk/index/210730/) | "You won't believe this" < a named, specific gap. |
| **1.11 The package gap is scored on watch time.** YouTube's title/thumbnail test picks the winner by watch time "over other metrics, like click-through-rate". | A gap the video does not pay off loses on YouTube's own metric. | PRACTITIONER (T1). [YouTube Help 13861714](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714) (read 2026-10-12) | Confirm the title/thumbnail promise early (see 2.7 and 9.4). |

### A2. Suspense and anticipation (item 2)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2.1 Structural-affect theory.** The order of events picks the emotion. Suspense: initiating event early, outcome withheld, chronological. Surprise: crucial information withheld, then revealed unexpectedly. Curiosity: a significant early event is omitted, the audience knows it is missing, and later information lets them reconstruct it. | The agent can choose the emotion by choosing the order. | THEORY. [Brewer & Lichtenstein 1982](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-2166(82)90021-2) ([tech report](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/17648)) | Cold open with the result → curiosity. Countdown in real time → suspense. Hidden fact revealed late → surprise. |
| **2.2 Affective disposition.** Suspense is concern for a *liked* protagonist facing an uncertain outcome; the theory predicts more suspense when a bad outcome is likely. | No one to root for → little suspense. | THEORY (second-hand; primary chapters not read). Zillmann via [Kaspar et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5156838/) and [Chun et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.558234/full) | Establish whom the viewer roots for. On a faceless walk, the protagonist is the viewer's own stake (will I get there, will it be worth it). |
| **2.3 Suspense in non-fiction (news).** Three experiments (n = 263, 255, 599): perceived liking of the protagonist (all) and perceived likelihood of a **good** ending (Studies 2–3) correlated with suspense. Suspense correlated with appreciation (r ≈ .32–.50) and lingering interest (r ≈ .47–.58). Manipulated liking and outcome had weak or inconsistent effects. | Contradicts 2.2 on outcome direction: hope may beat dread. | THEORY (text news). [Kaspar, Zimmermann & Wilbers 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5156838/) | In non-fiction, frame the uncertain outcome as something to hope for. Test how viewers read the protagonist; intent is not enough. |
| **2.4 Anomalous suspense.** Readers of suspenseful texts about known historical events were slower to verify the true outcome. | A known outcome can still grip. | THEORY. [Gerrig 1989](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/suspense-in-the-absence-of-uncertainty/) | A title that gives away the ending can still carry suspense if the telling puts the viewer inside the moment of uncertainty. |
| **2.5 Known-outcome suspense is weaker.** Three viewings of the same film chase (each with a different ending, n = 49): suspense 6.80 → 5.08 → 4.69 (−31%), arousal 7.16 → 6.08; enjoyment unchanged. | Rewatch or spoiled suspense keeps about two-thirds of its strength. | DATA (lab, film). [Chun, Park & Shi 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.558234/full) | Do not rely on known-outcome suspense alone; add new uncertainty (how, at what cost). |
| **2.6 Suspense and surprise formalised.** Suspense = variance of next period's beliefs; surprise = size of the belief change. Optimal information-release schedules are derived (mysteries, game shows, sports). | A reveal plan should keep the *next* step uncertain and occasionally move belief sharply. | THEORY (abstract only). [Ely, Frankel & Kamenica 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/doi10.1086-677350.html) | Each segment should leave the next outcome open. Schedule at least one belief reversal (a twist). |
| **2.7 Domain-general tension.** Conflict, instability, dissonance or uncertainty trigger prediction of emotionally weighted future events, in music, film and narrative alike. | Script suspense and musical tension are the same mechanism. | THEORY. [Lehne & Koelsch 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00079) | Build musical tension under narrative uncertainty; release both together (see A7). |
| **2.8 Reveal pacing.** Curiosity peaks before the reveal and may turn aversive (1.8); teased resolution beats all-at-once (1.7). | Step reveals hold; a stall at the last step costs goodwill. | DATA (lab) / THEORY. Hsiung 2023; Ruan 2018 | Reveal in steps (partial, then full). No delay after the final setup. |
| **2.9 Re-engagement by rule change.** Twist or escalation about every 3 min, e.g. a new rule in a challenge. | Uncertainty is renewed periodically. | PRACTITIONER. MrBeast doc via [OMR](https://omr.com/de/daily/mrbeast-doc-pdf-leak), [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production) (2024-09) | In long-form, plan a new rule, twist or escalation every few minutes. No interval has been tested (C). |
| **2.10 Cliffhangers.** Episodes ending on a cliffhanger raised arousal (self-report and skin conductance) but not enjoyment or intention to keep watching (N = 133, 3–4 drama episodes). Need for closure, sensation seeking and need for cognition did not moderate it. | Ending on an unresolved hook does not raise intention to watch the next one. | DATA (lab, TV). [Wirz, Ort, Rasch & Fahr 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000392) ([accepted ms](https://sonar.ch/documents/319990/files/2022_Wirz_Role.pdf)) | Close the video's own loop. Do not count on "tease the next video" at the end. |

### A3. Surprise, novelty and pattern interrupts (item 3)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **3.1 Orienting response and habituation.** A change from the brain's current model triggers orienting; repetition weakens it until it stops; significant or emotional stimuli habituate more slowly. | A repeated device (same transition, same zoom, same whoosh) loses its attention value. | THEORY. Sokolov 1960 / Bradley 2009 via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orienting_response); [Rankin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2008.09.012) | Vary the *kind* of change, not only its frequency. |
| **3.2 Cuts trigger involuntary orienting.** Structural features of TV (cuts, edits) trigger cardiac orienting; mild emotional content intensifies it. | A cut is an automatic attention pulse. | DATA (lab, TV ads). [Lang 1990](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365090017003001) | Cuts buy attention; emotional content buys more per cut. |
| **3.3 Related vs unrelated cuts.** Both orient. After unrelated cuts (scene and content change) reaction times slowed and memory for what followed was worse, especially visual memory; related cuts improved it. | A hard topic jump costs encoding. | DATA (lab, TV). [Lang, Geiger, Strickwerda & Sumner 1993](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365093020001001) | Prefer cuts that keep the topic and change the view. Do not place key information right after a topic jump. |
| **3.4 Edit rate within a scene.** Faster camera changes inside one scene raised physiological and self-reported arousal and **memory**, "without significantly increasing the cognitive load". | Jump-cut pacing inside one scene helps. | DATA (lab, TV). [Lang, Zhou, Schwartz, Bolls & Potter 2000](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15506878jobem4401_7) | Best support for tight jump cuts in talking-head video. |
| **3.5 Overload.** Fast pace and arousing content each raise allocated resources; combined, they overload, and recognition and cued recall drop. | More stimulation is not monotonic. | DATA (lab, TV). [Lang, Bolls, Potter & Kawahara 1999](https://doi.org/10.1080/08838159909364504) | Slow the edit during emotionally intense or information-dense moments; cut faster on calm stretches. |
| **3.6 Audio interrupts.** Voice changes in radio messages produced orienting that did not habituate over 2 min, plus momentary overload that grew with the number of changes (n = 62). | A sound or voice change resets attention, at a cost when frequent. | THEORY (radio). [Potter 2000](https://doi.org/10.1207/S1532785XMEP0202_3) | Use a voice or sound change as a reset, sparingly. |
| **3.7 Bayesian surprise drives gaze.** Change against expectation predicted gaze in video better than 10 other metrics (~20% above saliency; >72% of 10,192 saccades landed on more-surprising locations). White noise had ~20× more information but ~17× less surprise than TV. | Attention follows unexpected change, not busyness. | DATA (lab, video eye tracking; 8 observers, 50 clips). [Itti & Baldi 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2782645/) | Do not add random visual noise to "hold the eye". Change something the viewer did not predict. |
| **3.8 Edit blindness.** A quarter of continuity cuts between viewpoints went unnoticed, a third when the cut coincided with motion onset. | Smooth cuts are not interrupts. | DATA (lab, feature film). [Smith & Henderson 2008](https://doi.org/10.16910/jemr.2.2.6) | An intended interrupt must break continuity or change content (paying 3.3's cost). |
| **3.9 Pace in waves.** Across 150 Hollywood films (1935–2005), shot lengths became correlated with neighbours, approaching 1/f; authors suggest this harnesses attention. | Pace should vary in runs, not hold a fixed rate. | THEORY (corpus, no viewer measure). [Cutting, DeLong & Nothelfer 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610361679) | Alternate runs of short shots with runs of long ones. |
| **3.10 Visual-mode alternation.** In 6.9M edX sessions, videos alternating talking head with slides or code were more engaging than slides alone; median engagement ≤6 min whatever the length. | Changing visual mode is a measured engagement lever in explanatory video. | DATA (field, MOOC; 2014, old). [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://pg.ucsd.edu/publications/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf) | Alternate face, B-roll and screen at segment turns. |
| **3.11 Dispersed gaze predicts exits.** Across 31 ads (~2,000 viewers), attention scattered across viewers predicted zapping; "pulsing" the brand reduced avoidance at equal exposure (confirmed with edited ads). | Moments with no focal point lose viewers. | DATA (lab, video ads). [Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2010](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1100.0567) | Every shot needs one clear focal point. A recurring element should pulse in and out rather than sit on screen. |
| **3.12 Stimulation has an optimum.** Across 82 ads (178 viewers, facial tracking), entertainment had an inverted-U relation to purchase intent; entertainment after the brand helped, before it did not. | Constant "fun" interrupts crowd out the point. | DATA (field, video ads). [Teixeira, Picard & el Kaliouby 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2014.0854) | Cap gags and interrupts; put the point first, then the fun. |
| **3.13 Content change as interrupt.** "As soon as one storyline slows, introduce something new"; an "element of surprise" (house lowered by crane ~30 s in); re-engagement at ~3 and ~6 min. | Practitioners interrupt with new rules, contests or locations, not only visual change. | PRACTITIONER. MrBeast doc via [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production), [Chewning](https://zackchewning.substack.com/p/content-lessons-from-the-1-youtubers) | Script interrupts as content events (new rule, place, person), which are "related cuts" in Lang's sense. |

### A4. Emotional arcs, contagion, valence and arousal; sharing versus watch time (item 4)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4.1 Six core arcs.** Sentiment series of 1,327 Gutenberg novels decompose into Rags to riches, Riches to rags, Man in a hole, Icarus, Cinderella, Oedipus (three methods agree). The most common arcs are not the most downloaded: Icarus (median 97), Oedipus (94) and double Man in a hole (105.5) beat pure rise or fall (80–83). Authors: downloads are "only a rough proxy". | Arcs with more turns may beat monotonic ones. | THEORY (text, measured). [Reagan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjds/s13688-016-0093-1) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07772)). Notes tag DATA (text) | Use the six shapes as beat-sheet vocabulary, not a recipe. Prefer shapes with at least two turns. |
| **4.2 Man in a hole earns most at the box office.** 6,147 film scripts (3,051 with budget and gross; the JORS abstract says 6,174) cluster into the same six shapes. Man in a hole earns the most regardless of genre or budget, but is "talked about", not the best rated. A successful Riches-to-rags film is an epic with a >$100M budget. | Fall-then-rise has the strongest single-shape evidence for reach, but it is film evidence and not liking. | THEORY (scripts + revenue, measured). [Del Vecchio et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/01605682.2019.1705194); [Birmingham summary](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/latest/2018/07/data-science-and-movies.aspx) | Default spine for reach: trouble, then recovery. |
| **4.3 Normative narrative structure.** Staging early, progression through the middle, cognitive tension peaking near the climax; following the norm was **not** linked to popularity. | Structure is measurable but not a success predictor. | THEORY (text, measured). [Boyd, Blackburn & Pennebaker 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aba2196) | Do not force a three-act template for its own sake. |
| **4.4 Speed and scope.** In ~50,000 texts (movie and TV subtitles), stories that move faster through semantic space are liked more; TV episodes that cover more ground are liked less. | Fast progression through few topics wins. | THEORY (subtitles, measured). [Toubia, Berger & Eliashberg 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2011695118) | Move quickly through one idea; do not widen scope. Strongest for short-form. |
| **4.5 Sentiment volatility.** Tone swings predict higher ratings across 4,000+ movies (stronger for thrillers) and continued reading across 30,000+ articles; experiments show stimulation is the cause. | Alternating good and bad beats holds attention. | THEORY (text + lab, measured). [Berger, Kim & Meyer 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab010) | Alternate positive and negative beats; do not front-load all the good ones. |
| **4.6 Rollercoaster and end effect (video).** 120 ads, 43,000+ moment ratings: likeability at the start and especially at the **end** raises intent to share; a linear trend has no effect; variance raises virality; the peak matters once overall liking is controlled. | Open strong, vary, end on a high. | DATA (survey, video ads; 2016, old). [Shehu, Bijmolt & Clement 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intmar.2016.01.001) | A steady climb buys nothing by itself. End on the peak. |
| **4.7 Surprise level and joy velocity.** Facial and eye tracking plus zapping every 250 ms (58 adults, 28 ads): surprise *level* concentrates attention; joy *velocity* retains, gains outweigh losses; surprise is hard to sustain past ~1 s; several short joy bursts beat one long stretch; alternating emotions counters adaptation. | Retention follows rising joy, not joy held flat. | DATA (lab, video ads; 2012, old as a platform finding). [Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0207); [HBS summary](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/creating-online-ads-we-want-to-watch) | Open with a short surprise; deliver joy as repeated small rises. A punchline at the end is wasted on viewers who left. |
| **4.8 Valence × arousal.** Affect sits on two axes; discrete emotions are positions on the circle. | Sharing and retention depend on arousal (4.10–4.13), not valence alone. | THEORY ◦. [Russell 1980](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077714) | Label every beat on both axes in the script. |
| **4.9 Emotional contagion.** People mimic expressions, voices and postures and converge emotionally. Through a **text** feed the effect is tiny: N = 689,003 Facebook users, d = 0.02 (0.1 and 0.07 percentage points); PNAS added an expression of concern about consent. | The on-screen face, voice and laughter are the plausible channel; text alone is not. | THEORY ◦. [Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8721.ep10770953). THEORY (text feed, measured; notes: DATA field). [Kramer, Guillory & Hancock 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4066473/) | Show the emotion (a face, a real laugh, a voice) rather than captioning it. |
| **4.10 High-arousal emotions drive sharing.** ~7,000 NYT articles, odds of the most-emailed list per +1 SD: anger +34%, awe +30%, practical value +30%, interest +25%, anxiety +21%, surprise +14%, positivity +13%, sadness −16%. Lab: amusement and anger raise sharing intent, sadness lowers it, arousal mediates. | Shares come from awe, amusement, anger, anxiety; not from sadness. | THEORY (text, field + lab; 2008 data). [Berger & Milkman 2012](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353) | Where the goal is shares, aim for high arousal. See C for the failed replication of the arousal mechanism. |
| **4.11 Video sharing follows arousal.** ~800 videos with actual daily shares: high arousal is the main driver; valence matters less. Across YouTube ads (two field studies + holdout): amusement, excitement, inspiration and warmth raise sharing; information focus lowers it; drama (surprise, plot, characters, babies, animals) produces those emotions; early or persistent branding hurts; sharing peaks at 1.2–1.7 min. | The text sharing results hold on video. | DATA (field, video). [Nelson-Field, Riebe & Newstead 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ausmj.2013.07.003); [Tellis et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242919841034) | For shareable cuts, lead with drama and positive arousal; keep information secondary. |
| **4.12 Clicks are not shares.** 105,000+ Upworthy variants (370M impressions): each negative word +2.3% CTR, each positive word −1.0%; per SD, sadness +0.7% odds of a click, joy −0.9%, fear −0.7%, anger no effect. Authors: consumption is private, sharing is public. | What wins the click (sad, negative) differs from what wins the share. | THEORY (text, field RCTs). [Robertson et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4); notes: arcs tag DATA (text), ethics retagged THEORY | Write separate rules for packaging (click) and content (share). |
| **4.13 What holds attention.** 600,000+ reading sessions over 35,000 pieces: anxious, exciting and hopeful language keeps people reading; sad language makes them stop. Changing a few words: 80% kept reading (anxious), 71% (angry), 52% (sad); uncertainty plus arousal mediate. The paper cites Pezzuti et al. 2021 (brand posts) that certain content gets more likes and shares but less sustained attention. | Retention needs uncertainty plus arousal; sadness and closure end sessions. | THEORY (text, field + lab). [Berger, Moe & Schweidel 2023](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/main-paper.pdf); [AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/2023/03/21/the-challenge-of-keeping-an-audience-engaged-how-language-shapes-attention/) | Every middle segment leaves something uncertain and arousing (hope, excitement, worry). Sadness and closure go at the end. |
| **4.14 Platforms weigh different signals.** YouTube counts only videos rated 4–5 stars as "valued watchtime"; people are "more likely to be satisfied by videos that they share or like", weighted per user. Shorts lead Todd Sherman: likes, comments, shares are "a decent amount of signal" but sparse; viewed-vs-swiped, AVD and watch time exist on every video. Jenny Hoyos's team correlated views with every metric: likes, comments, shares "didn't really affect video performance". Instagram: "The most important predictions … reshare a reel, watch a reel all the way through, like it, and go to the audio page." | Shares matter more on Reels than on YouTube. | PRACTITIONER (T1). ### A5. Parasocial relationships and trust (item 5)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5.1 Parasocial interaction (PSI) vs relationship (PSR).** Media create an "illusion of face-to-face relationship". PSI (mutual awareness during one viewing) and PSR (the bond across viewings) are different constructs (N = 383 scale comparison). | Retention inside a video and returning across videos are separate design problems. | THEORY ◦. [Horton & Wohl 1956](https://doi.org/10.1080/00332747.1956.11023049); THEORY. [Dibble, Hartmann & Rosaen 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/hcre.12063) | PSI: address and gaze. PSR: consistency and disclosure across videos. |
| **5.2 Bodily + verbal address.** 2 × 2 experiment (N = 198): PSI is strongest when the performer faces the camera **and** says "you"; stronger PSI → more enjoyment. | With no face, verbal address is the half that remains. | DATA (lab, video). [Hartmann & Goldhoorn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2011.01595.x) | Talk to the lens; say "you". In faceless VO, keep second person. |
| **5.3 Homophily, not loneliness.** Meta-analysis, k = 120: no link between loneliness and PSR; PSR correlates strongly with homophily, identification, transportation and persuasion. | "People like me" builds the bond. | DATA (meta-analysis). [Tukachinsky, Walter & Saucier 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqaa034) | Show the author's ordinary life and places the viewer could visit. |
| **5.4 Comments do not build the bond.** On YouTube, PSR strength is not much influenced by use of comments and likes. Fairness in how an influencer interacts, and similarity, do raise PSR. | Comment activity is not the lever; how the creator treats people is. | DATA (survey). [Rihl & Wegener 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354856517736976); [Yuan & Lou 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/15252019.2020.1769514) | Do not script comment-bait to build trust (see also 9.6). |
| **5.5 Self-disclosure.** N = 433: intimate self-disclosure raises purchase intent via PSR, credibility and trust; PSR beats source credibility. | Disclosure builds the bond without a face. | DATA (survey). [Leite & Baptista 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/10696679.2021.1935275) | Add one personal line per video (VO or text card). |
| **5.6 The voice carries trust.** Podcast listeners (n = 804) form PSR from audio alone. Recruiters rated a candidate more competent and intelligent when they **heard** the pitch than when they read it; adding video did not change ratings. Hearing a view expressed makes the speaker seem more human; pitch variation and pauses carry it. | A natural voice is the best substitute for a face. | DATA (survey; lab). [Schlütz & Hedder 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/19376529.2020.1870467); [Schroeder & Epley 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615572906); [Schroeder, Kardas & Epley 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617713798) | Prefer the author's own voice with natural intonation over captions alone or flat TTS (extrapolation). |
| **5.7 A face buys liking, not comprehension.** edX: talking-head interleave beat slides only; informal recordings beat studio; faster, enthusiastic speakers held longer. Eye tracking: the face takes ~41% of viewing time and raises affect and satisfaction but **not** recall. | Show the face for warmth at turning points, not for clarity. | DATA (field, MOOC; lab + eye tracking; 2014, old). [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556325.2566239); [Kizilcec et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557207) | Cut to the face at hook, reveal and end; keep production informal. |
| **5.8 One-and-a-half-sided streams.** In live streaming the creator can actually respond, so the relationship stops being purely parasocial. | Live replies move PSR toward a real relationship. | THEORY (conceptual). [Kowert & Daniel 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2021.100150) | Out of scope for edited Reels; noted for completeness. |
| **5.9 The bond is also a distribution signal; templates are a risk.** Instagram ranks on "your history of interacting with the person who posted". YouTube's 2025-07-15 "inauthentic content" rule demonetises "content that looks like it's made with a template", "scrolling text with minimal or no narrative, commentary, or educational value", and mass-produced AI content; reused content that "primarily features the uploading creator" is allowed. The page does not mention faceless content. | Faceless is fine; voiceless, templated footage is the risk. | PRACTITIONER (T1). [Mosseri 2023](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained/) (old); [YouTube Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392) | Every video carries narration, commentary or a personal point of view, and does not reuse one template. |
| **5.10 Practitioner authenticity signals.** Ryan Trahan: "if you just put a camera on an everyday person … everyone would fall in love with them"; chose donations over a sponsorship that "wouldn't come across as genuine"; "99%" iPhone with native phone UI graphics, less conspicuous in public. Kallaway: trust = "time under trust", built by "non-obvious and tactically useful" content from "real skills, real experiences, real proof"; superfans need relatability, transformation, expertise and personality. Jordan Matter: reality scaling and relationship content "exist in conflict". MrBeast doc: "I'm not fake and I will be authentic". | — | PRACTITIONER. Colin and Samir [CQE2E88QJes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CQE2E88QJes) 14:17–14:42, 16:51–17:43, 18:18–19:45 (2025-07-13); Kallaway [DFSU-YPRTs8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DFSU-YPRTs8) 1:01–2:58 (2026-05-25), [6G1wIdxWF5w](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6G1wIdxWF5w) 10:53–12:45 (2026-06-10); [d18ud-4epP8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d18ud-4epP8) 9:32–10:26 (2026-02-04); MrBeast PDF p. 25 (all auto-captions) | For a POV travel walk: phone-native look, real strangers, real places. Avoid manufactured conflict on a relationship-led channel. |

### A6. Humor and relief (item 6)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **6.1 Benign violation.** Amusement comes from a violation seen at the same time as benign (alternative norm, weak commitment, psychological distance). Five experiments on moral violations. | A joke needs a real "wrong" and a reason it is OK. | THEORY / DATA (lab, text scenarios). [McGraw & Warren 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610376073) | A travel mishap is funny when the stakes are clearly low. |
| **6.2 Humor as a reset.** Humor reduces negative emotion through cognitive distraction (working-memory account). | A light beat after a heavy one lets tension be built again. | DATA (lab). [Strick et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015951) | After a tense or sad beat, put a light one before the next build. |
| **6.3 Timing: the beat after.** In 1,200 natural laughter episodes, >99% of laughs fell in pauses at phrase ends ("speech has priority access"). In 20 joke performances, speakers did not slow down and there was no evidence of a pause **before** punchlines. | Room after the punchline matters; a pause before it is not what speakers do. | DATA (field observation). [Provine 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1439-0310.1993.tb00478.x). DATA (small sample). [Attardo & Pickering 2011](https://doi.org/10.1515/humr.2011.015) | Leave a beat after the punchline with no VO, SFX or caption on top. |
| **6.4 Laughter is contagious, if real and from "us".** Laughter triggers laughs and smiles (title-level check only). Added laughter makes jokes funnier; spontaneous beats posed. Canned laughter helps only when the audience is seen as the in-group. | Real laughter of people the viewer identifies with is a lever. | DATA (lab). [Provine 1992](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03330380); [Cai et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.05.073); [Platow et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2004.09.005) | Keep the author's or companions' real laughter in the mix; no canned laughter. |
| **6.5 Distinctiveness.** Humorous sentences are recalled better only when mixed with non-humorous ones. | One joke in a sincere video is memorable; an all-joke video loses the edge. | DATA (lab, text). [Schmidt 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.20.4.953) | Ration jokes. |
| **6.6 Amusement drives sharing.** Amusement raised sharing intent in the lab (an ad campaign; F(1,47) = 10.89), mediated by arousal; it is one of four emotions that raise sharing of YouTube ads. | Comedy is a share lever. | THEORY (text lab) [Berger & Milkman 2012](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353); DATA (field, video) [Tellis et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242919841034) | For Reels, a funny payoff is a share trigger. |
| **6.7 No universal humor effect.** Review of 250+ works: effectiveness depends on type of humor, audience, product, and relatedness to the message. | — | THEORY (review). [Weinberger & Gulas 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/02650487.2019.1598831) | Keep humor related to the point. |
| **6.8 Practitioner comedy craft.** Hoyos: Shorts are "bits" ("That's a funny joke — and onto the next video"); cramming information "dilute[s] your message". Josh Johnson: laughs come from recognition, surprise, discomfort, strong agreement; one lane makes you "a caricature"; "if people don't get the joke, you … didn't do your job"; room laughter works as social proof. Kate Tolo: humor needs "a lot of post-production … comedic timing". MrBeast doc: "funny sound effects" make a simple game funny. | — | PRACTITIONER. Creator Insider [_tWy-_otnUc](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_tWy-_otnUc) 5:11–5:41, 8:23–8:54; Colin and Samir [cm2ZzY_x9eg](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cm2ZzY_x9eg) 44:17–46:04, 37:29–37:59, 35:19–35:47 (2025-08-12); [Ak-YNIBqEjI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ak-YNIBqEjI) 40:54–41:01; MrBeast PDF p. 26–27 (auto-captions) | Never explain a joke afterwards in a caption; rewrite the setup. Treat timing as an edit task. |

### A7. Music and sound (item 7)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **7.1 Two separate levers.** The same Mozart piece at four tempo × mode versions: tempo changed arousal, not mood; mode changed mood, not arousal. The "Mozart effect" disappears once arousal and mood are held constant. | No track is magic; it works through arousal and valence. | THEORY. [Husain, Thompson & Schellenberg 2002](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2002.20.2.151); [Thompson, Schellenberg & Husain 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00345) | Pick tempo for energy and key/mode for valence, per section of the video. |
| **7.2 Silence lowers arousal.** A random 2-min pause after music lowered heart rate, blood pressure and ventilation **below** the pre-music baseline (n = 24). | Silence is an active contrast, not a neutral gap. | THEORY (physiology, music only). [Bernardi, Porta & Sleight 2006](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600) | Use a drop to silence as a breath before the next hit. No video test exists (C). |
| **7.3 Music sets the reading of footage.** Positive or negative music on ambiguous film clips biased interpretation of characters, ending guesses and memory a week later, mood-congruently. Music before or after a neutral face shifted how viewers read its emotion (n = 177). | On a voiceless POV walk, the track supplies much of the meaning. A cue just before or after a shot recolours it. | DATA (lab, film). [Boltz 2001](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2001.18.4.427); [Tan, Spackman & Bezdek 2007](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.25.2.135) | Choose the track for the reading you want. A sting can sit just before or after the shot, not only under it. |
| **7.4 Foreshadow vs accompany.** Music that foreshadows an event aids memory when mood-*incongruent*; music that accompanies an event aids it when mood-*congruent*. | Contrast before a reveal, match during it. | DATA (lab, film). [Boltz, Schulkind & Kantra 1991](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03197154) | Before the reveal, play against it; at the reveal, match it. |
| **7.5 Peaks come from violated expectation.** >80% of 83 listeners reported chills, tears or laughter; shivers came with new or unexpected harmonies, tears with sequences and appoggiaturas. Dopamine is released at the peak (nucleus accumbens) and during anticipation (caudate). | Build-ups have reward value of their own. | THEORY. [Sloboda 1991](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735691192002); THEORY ◦. [Salimpoor et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726) | Keep a riser before a drop or reveal even when the payoff is short. Peaks need a harmonic or rhythmic surprise, not just volume. |
| **7.6 Six induction mechanisms.** Brain-stem reflex, evaluative conditioning, contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory, musical expectancy. Studies that ignore the mechanism get inconsistent results. Familiar pop excerpts activated limbic and reward regions more than unfamiliar ones; familiarity mattered more than liking. | A hit on a cut and a nostalgic song work through different mechanisms. | THEORY. [Juslin & Västfjäll 2008](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08005293); THEORY ◦. [Pereira et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0027241) | Each music rule names its mechanism. Familiar audio has a mechanism-level case; platform evidence is missing (C). |
| **7.7 Event boundaries.** People perceive boundaries at perceptual prediction errors, which regulate attention and memory. In film, cuts that break **action** continuity drive segmentation; space/time-only cuts have minor effects. | A music change is a cheap boundary cue; it helps when it marks a real content change. | THEORY ◦. [Zacks et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.2.273); DATA (lab, film + fMRI). [Magliano & Zacks 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2011.01202.x) | Change the music where the content changes (new segment, reveal), not on every cut. |
| **7.8 Music competes with narration.** Background music and/or SFX added to a narrated animation reduced retention and transfer (coherence effect). | Under dense VO, music costs comprehension. | DATA (lab, narrated animation). [Moreno & Mayer 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.117) | Duck the music hard or drop it under dense VO; bring it up in the gaps. |
| **7.9 Mirroring music is forgettable.** Film-craft analysis: music that mirrors the picture is safe and forgettable; removing a Marvel cue leaves the scene unchanged; narration over a cue "distracts us from the emotion of the scene". | Music should add what the picture lacks. | PRACTITIONER (analyst; 2016, old for platforms, film craft). [Every Frame a Painting](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7vfqkvwW2fs) | No VO over the emotional peak of a cue. Use counterpoint or foreshadowing (7.3, 7.4). |

### A8. Cognitive load (item 8)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **8.1 About 4 chunks.** Working memory holds ~4 chunks (3–5); Miller's "7 ± 2" was a rough estimate. | More than ~4 new items live at once will drop. | THEORY. [Cowan 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922) | At most 3–4 new names, numbers or steps per segment; chunk or show the rest. |
| **8.2 One budget for edits and content.** LC4MP: cuts and content draw on the same limited resources; fast pace + arousing content overloads (3.5). | Edit density and information density spend the same budget. | THEORY ◦. [Lang 2000](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2000.tb02833.x); DATA (lab, TV). [Lang et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1080/08838159909364504) | At a shock, reveal or dense passage: slow the cut and drop the information load. |
| **8.3 Speech rate × density.** "The higher the speed, the lower the information density should be": recognition peaked at ~170 wpm for dense and ~190 wpm for light content. In edX, engagement rose with speaking rate (Brame's suggested range 185–254 wpm is a review suggestion, not a tested optimum). Lecture video at 1.5× or 2× cost little comprehension; beyond 2× it dropped. | Viewers absorb faster speech than assumed; density sets the ceiling. | THEORY (radio). [Rodero 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2014.1002942); DATA (field, MOOC; old). [Guo et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556325.2566239) via [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/); DATA (lab, lecture video). [Murphy et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3899) | VO at ~170 wpm on fact-heavy lines, up to ~190 on connective lines. |
| **8.4 Redundancy and seductive details.** On-screen text that duplicated or summarised narration lowered retention and transfer; interesting but irrelevant inserts lowered transfer. | Full VO sentences on screen over busy footage compete. | DATA (lab, narrated animation). [Mayer, Heiser & Lonn 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.1.187) | No transcript-style text over B-roll; every insert serves its segment's point. |
| **8.5 Keywords help.** 2–3 printed words identical to the narration, placed next to the relevant picture, improved retention (d = 0.47, 0.70), not transfer. | Labels guide attention; transcripts split it. | DATA (lab, narrated slides). [Mayer & Johnson 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380) | On-screen text = a keyword or short phrase, placed on the thing named. |
| **8.6 Spoken + written can win.** Meta-analysis of 57 studies: spoken+written beat spoken-only for low-prior-knowledge learners, system-paced material and picture-free material; key terms beat verbatim. 100+ studies: same-language captions improve comprehension, attention and memory, especially for non-native and D/deaf viewers. | Platform video is system-paced with a novice audience, which argues for text, conditionally. | DATA (meta-analysis, mostly lab). [Adesope & Nesbit 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147); DATA (review). [Gernsbacher 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215602130) | Talking head with little imagery: captions. Dense B-roll: keywords only. International or sound-off: captions. |
| **8.7 Segment, signal, weed.** Together they improved transfer and lowered perceived difficulty; learner-paced segmenting improved transfer. | One idea per segment with a visible boundary helps. | DATA (lab, educational video). [Ibrahim et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2011.585993); [Mayer & Chandler 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.390) | One idea per segment; mark the boundary (card, music change, "second thing"); build a beat of silence or a held shot after each dense idea, since the viewer cannot pause-pace. |
| **8.8 Modality and personalisation.** Narration + visuals beats printed text + visuals; conversational wording improves learning. | — | THEORY (review). [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/) | Explain in VO, show in picture, write in first and second person. |
| **8.9 Expertise reversal.** Load-reducing techniques can lose their benefit or hurt experienced learners. | Recaps and signposts bore an expert niche. | THEORY (review). [Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4) | Set density by audience, not by a fixed rule. |

### A9. Ethics and limits (item 9)

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence | Practical rule for a script or an edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **9.1 Valued watch time.** YouTube surveys viewers (1–5 stars), counts only 4–5 star videos as "valued watchtime" and predicts survey answers for everyone else; "not all watchtime is equal"; dislikes are negative; shares from people who share everything are discounted. | **9.2 Borderline demotion.** Demotion gave "a 70% drop in watchtime on non-subscribed, recommended borderline content in the U.S."; target <0.5% of views. 2019: affected "less than one percent of the content". | Sensational borderline content loses recommendation reach. | DATA (platform-reported, method not shown). [YouTube 2019](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/continuing-our-work-to-improve/) (old) | Stay out of borderline claims (miracle cures, conspiracies). |

[GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production); PRACTITIONER (creator's before/after graphs on a few videos; 2021, old). [Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S2xHZPH5Sng) | Prefer a clear, specific promise over a hype word. |
| **9.5 Engagement bait is detected, also in audio.** Meta demotes "LIKE this if…" bait in posts (2017), comments (2018) and spoken in video audio (2019-01-30); repeat offenders get page-level demotion. Meta 2024 creator guidance lists clickbait and engagement bait as disliked and unoriginal reposts as low quality. | Spoken "comment X if…" lines in VO can be demoted. | PRACTITIONER (T1). [Meta Newsroom 2017, updated 2019](https://about.fb.com/news/2017/12/news-feed-fyi-fighting-engagement-bait-on-facebook/) (old); [Meta LatAm 2024-05](https://about.fb.com/ltam/news/2024/05/ayudando-a-los-creadores-a-encontrar-nuevas-audiencias/) | Ask for a real opinion, never a mechanical reaction. |
| **9.6 Templates are demonetised.** YouTube's 2025-07-15 "inauthentic content" rule covers content that "looks like it's made with a template" and mass-produced AI content (see 5.9). | Agent scripts that converge on one template are a policy risk. | PRACTITIONER (T1). [YouTube Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392) | Vary structure across videos; keep the author's commentary. |
| **9.7 Negativity, out-group and moral emotion buy reach.** Negative words raise CTR (4.12). Each moral-emotional word raised diffusion ~20% across 563,312 tweets, mostly within groups. Out-group posts were shared ~2× as often (each term +67% odds, ~4.8× the effect of negative affect) across 2.73M posts and predicted "angry" reactions. | Outrage spreads, mostly inside one camp. | THEORY (text, measured). [Brady et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1618923114); [Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2024292118).
