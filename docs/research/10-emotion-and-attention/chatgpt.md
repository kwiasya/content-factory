# Emotion and the Psychology of Attention in Video

## Evidence key and scope

The most important conclusion is that **the strongest science does not support a formulaic “retention hack” model**. Curiosity, suspense, surprise, arousal, social presence and cognitive load are real mechanisms, but the research rarely yields universal numbers such as “three open loops,” “a cut every three seconds,” “a reveal every 30 seconds,” or “180 words per minute.” Where those numbers appear in creator advice, they are usually extrapolations rather than experimentally established video laws. Loewenstein’s information-gap theory, for example, explains *why* a missing answer can become motivationally salient, but it does not specify a YouTube-compatible gap duration; multimedia-learning research establishes capacity constraints, but not a maximum number of narrative loops.

**DATA** below means a measurement or experiment was actually performed. I specify when the data come from video, another medium, or a laboratory analogue. **THEORY** means a psychological or narrative model from which a video prediction is being derived. **PRACTITIONER** is reserved for creator/analyst practice without causal data. I found much stronger primary psychological evidence than clean 2024–2026 creator self-experiments on these mechanisms; rather than laundering creator folklore into “data,” I exclude practitioner claims that did not meet the requested source bar.

For platform claims, the strongest current source located is YouTube’s own recommendation documentation. As of 2026 YouTube says its recommendation system has two explicit goals: finding videos viewers want to watch **and maximizing long-term viewer satisfaction**. Its listed signals include how much viewers watch, likes, shares, comments, “Not interested” feedback and survey responses. It also warns that repeatedly publishing content that does not resonate can affect a channel over time. That is materially different from the folklore model “maximize watch time at any cost.”

## Table A — mechanisms, predictions and rules

| Mechanism | What it predicts for video | Evidence and key source | Practical rule for script or edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity: information gap** | Curiosity becomes stronger when viewers become consciously aware that there is specific information they do not possess. A gap therefore needs two components: enough context to understand *what is missing*, and enough perceived value to care about resolving it. | **THEORY — Loewenstein, 1994.** Curiosity is framed as a deprivation state created by awareness of a gap in knowledge. **DATA — Frede et al., 2026.** Two preregistered experiments found that explicitly illustrating a knowledge gap increased curiosity and information seeking. | **THEORY/DATA-derived:** Do not begin with merely “something surprising is coming.” Establish the known state, then identify the missing piece: “We know X. The problem is Y. I tried Z to find out.” Make the question specific enough that the viewer can mentally represent the answer they are waiting for. |
| **Curiosity: gap magnitude** | Both trivial gaps and incomprehensibly large gaps can fail. Some discrepancy is needed, but a viewer also needs enough prior knowledge to recognize the discrepancy. | **THEORY.** Loewenstein reviews earlier discrepancy accounts in which intermediate discrepancy attracts more exploration than almost-zero or overwhelming discrepancy; newer work likewise treats prior knowledge/confidence as interacting with curiosity rather than implying “more uncertainty = more curiosity.” | **THEORY-derived:** Give the minimum orientation necessary before opening a technical mystery. “Here is the weird result” generally works better than asking a question whose vocabulary the viewer does not yet understand. |
| **Curiosity versus prediction error** | A violated prediction can create attention and surprise, but that is not identical to curiosity. Surprise is an immediate response to unexpected input; curiosity requires motivation to acquire missing information. | **THEORY + DATA.** Contemporary curiosity work supports relationships among uncertainty, knowledge and information seeking, while auditory prediction-violation research shows phasic arousal specifically to violated regularities. The two processes overlap but should not be collapsed into one variable. | **THEORY-derived:** Use prediction error to *open* a question—show something that contradicts expectation—then state or imply the information gap that lets curiosity sustain attention after the surprise has faded. |
| **Multiple open loops** | Extra unresolved questions create prospective goals, but every additional question also competes for limited processing capacity. The research does **not** establish that viewers can maintain exactly three, five or any other fixed number of loops. | **THEORY.** Multimedia-processing models assume limited capacity in both auditory/verbal and visual/pictorial channels. Information-gap theory itself provides no numeric “open-loop capacity.” | **THEORY-derived:** Maintain **one clearly dominant unresolved question**. Secondary questions are safest when nested under it rather than unrelated. Do not encode “maximum three loops” as a rule; encode “penalize simultaneous unrelated unresolved questions when they make the main objective harder to state.” |
| **How long a curiosity gap can remain open** | There is no experimentally validated number of seconds or percentage of a video after which an information gap “expires.” Tolerance should rise when progress is visible and fall when the viewer suspects deliberate withholding. | **THEORY / evidence gap.** Neither Loewenstein’s theory nor the video-adjacent studies located specify a universal duration. | **THEORY-derived:** Do not use time as the criterion. Use **progress**: if the main answer is delayed, each segment should either eliminate a possibility, raise the stakes, provide a partial answer, or materially change the viewer’s model. If several segments pass without progress, the gap has become withholding rather than suspense. |
| **Suspense: delayed uncertain outcome** | Suspense is strongest when an outcome matters, is unresolved, and the discourse postpones confirmation while providing relevant developments. | **THEORY — Brewer & Lichtenstein structural-affect theory.** Their model distinguishes suspense, surprise and curiosity by information order: suspense postpones an important outcome; curiosity reconstructs causes after an outcome is known; surprise introduces an unexpected event. | **THEORY-derived:** In challenges, investigations and experiments, establish the desired/feared result *before* procedural detail. Then let each beat change its probability: attempt → obstacle → adaptation → test → result. |
| **Suspense in nonfiction** | Suspense is not limited to fiction. News and factual material can produce suspense when the viewer cares about an unresolved outcome. | **DATA.** Experimental work on suspense in news narratives found that suspense can contribute to enjoyment of news stories, giving direct nonfiction support to the mechanism. | **DATA-derived:** A documentary/explainer does not need a fabricated cliffhanger. Real uncertainty is enough: “Will the test work?”, “Which explanation survives?”, “What did the investigation find?” Preserve genuine uncertainty rather than pretending the creator did not know information they obviously knew while editing. |
| **Anomalous suspense: known outcomes** | Knowing the ending does not necessarily eliminate suspense. Processing obstacles and simulating the unfolding path can temporarily re-create uncertainty even for historically known outcomes. | **DATA — Gerrig, 1989.** Experiments demonstrated “anomalous suspense,” including for outcomes participants already knew; obstacles could temporarily interfere with ready access to outcome knowledge. | **DATA-derived:** Spoiling the destination is not automatically fatal. A transformation video may reveal the “after” immediately and still retain through **how** it happened; history can announce the known outcome and make the route the uncertainty. |
| **Reveal pacing** | Delay creates suspense only while the delay contains outcome-relevant information. Delay by itself is not evidence of good pacing. | **THEORY + limited DATA.** Structural-affect theory predicts suspense from postponement, but the literature does not establish a universal optimal reveal interval. Film work on temporal expectancies suggests timing matters, without yielding a portable “reveal every X seconds” rule. | **THEORY-derived:** Delay a payoff only while you can show causal progress. Once the viewer possesses enough information to infer the answer confidently, either reveal it or introduce a *real* complication. |
| **Surprise and prediction violation** | Unexpected change can trigger rapid orienting/arousal. A change that is already predictable is less effective as an attention reset. | **DATA.** In rapid auditory sequences, pupil-linked phasic arousal was evoked by violations of established regularity rather than merely by the emergence of an expected new pattern. | **DATA-derived:** A pattern interrupt should actually violate the local pattern. After 20 identical zooms, the 21st zoom is no longer an interrupt. Sometimes the unexpected move is a locked-off shot, a sudden quiet moment, or an unusually long take. |
| **Editing and cut density** | Cuts can reorient attention and increase arousal, but more editing is not monotonically better. Effects depend on semantic continuity, spatial coherence and task demands. | **DATA.** Older television experiments found edit rate could increase arousal/attention and, over tested ranges, memory; research on edited dynamic scenes confirms that cuts interact with active visual attention. A 2025 VR experiment found editing could fragment attention and that unedited material sometimes produced stronger visual attention/immersion. | **DATA-derived:** Cut for a reason: new evidence, changed speaker, new spatial information, emotional emphasis, or compression of dead time. Do **not** target a cuts-per-minute number. Preserve continuity during material the viewer must understand. |
| **Overstimulation / constant interruption** | Increasing stimulus frequency can eventually compete with comprehension and prevent deep engagement. “Bored = needs more changes” is an unsafe diagnosis. | **DATA.** Dense “bullet-screen” interruptions impaired learning for some learners in a 2025 eye-tracking experiment. Separately, Tam et al.’s seven-experiment program found that switching and skipping online video often increased rather than reduced boredom; one broader-age replication did not show the same difference, so the effect is not universal. | **DATA-derived:** When retention drops, first test whether the section lacks meaning, progress or clarity—not whether it lacks motion. Alternate stimulation with periods of stable attention. |
| **Emotional arcs** | Viewers can track affective trajectories—rise, fall, recovery, reversal—but the famous “six arcs” are descriptive patterns in fiction, not six validated video-retention templates. | **DATA — Reagan et al., 2016.** Analysis of 1,327 Project Gutenberg works identified six common sentiment trajectories: rise, fall, fall–rise, rise–fall, rise–fall–rise and fall–rise–fall. The paper explicitly distinguishes emotional arc from plot. Popularity associations were also measured, but in books, not creator video. | **DATA-derived with domain caution:** Give a video an emotional *trajectory*, not a flat tone. A useful diagnostic is “Does the viewer’s expected emotional state change between beginning, middle and payoff?” Do not tell the agent that every video must instantiate one of Reagan’s six shapes. |
| **Arousal and sharing** | Activation matters strongly for transmission. High-arousal positive and negative emotions are more share-inducing than low-arousal states such as sadness, all else equal. | **DATA — Berger & Milkman, 2012.** Analysis of New York Times sharing plus experiments found greater transmission for high-arousal emotions such as awe, anger and anxiety and less for low-arousal sadness. **DATA — Nelson-Field et al., 2013.** Across two video datasets totaling about 800 commercial and noncommercial videos, emotional arousal was a major predictor of actual sharing; valence mattered less. | **DATA-derived:** If sharing is an objective, design at least one **high-arousal share moment**: awe, hilarity, astonishment, anger at a genuinely important injustice, or excitement. “Interesting” but emotionally flat information is less likely to travel. |
| **Valence versus arousal** | “Positive goes viral” and “negative goes viral” are both oversimplifications. Arousal often explains more than positive/negative valence, and replications show context-dependent effects. | **DATA.** Berger & Milkman and the viral-video study support arousal; cross-platform replication work finds many but not all original effects and suggests non-linear relationships with positivity. | **DATA-derived:** Tag emotion on **two axes** in the agent: valence and arousal. “Sad/calm,” “angry/activated,” “awed/activated” and “content/calm” should not be treated as equivalent merely because two are positive or negative. |
| **Sharing versus watch time** | An emotion that increases sharing need not maximize completion or long-term satisfaction. The viral-emotion literature is much stronger for sharing than for watch time. | **DATA + platform evidence.** The high-arousal studies measure sharing/virality, whereas current YouTube documentation optimizes a broader set of behaviors and explicitly names long-term satisfaction. | **DATA-derived:** Do not give anger, anxiety or outrage a generic “retention bonus.” Score separately: **likely share impulse**, **likely immediate attention**, **likely satisfaction/trust cost**. |
| **A face on screen / social presence** | A face is a strong social and visual cue and will attract processing resources, but “showing a face increases retention” is not a universal law. It can also compete with the material being explained. | **DATA — Gu et al., PNAS 2024.** On-screen instructor presence measurably changed learners’ visual attention and interbrain/neural synchrony during multimedia learning. Earlier eye-tracking work also reports mixed learning effects from instructor presence rather than a simple face advantage. | **DATA-derived:** Use the face when its expression, credibility or relationship is carrying information. During visually intricate explanations, shrink or remove it if viewers need the same screen area to inspect evidence. |
| **Parasocial relationship and trust** | Repeated exposure to a recognizable communicator can create a one-sided relational bond; credibility, perceived similarity and relational cues can strengthen that bond. The evidence for subsequent attitudes is better than direct causal evidence for minute-by-minute retention. | **DATA / THEORY.** Influencer research connects source/relational characteristics with parasocial relationships and downstream interest, but this literature should not be converted into “face = higher AVD.” | **DATA-derived, moderate confidence:** Preserve a stable human identity across videos—voice, worldview, recurring concerns, honest uncertainty and recognizable mannerisms. Optimize relationship consistency across uploads, not merely facial screen time within one upload. |
| **What substitutes when there is no face** | Voice, point of view and recognizable authorship can provide continuity, but there is weak evidence for a quantitative “voice substitutes X% of a face effect.” | **THEORY / evidence gap.** The direct face evidence shows social-presence effects, not equivalence among alternatives. | **THEORY-derived:** For faceless videos, maintain a consistent narrator voice/persona, first-person agency (“I tried…”, “we found…”), visible decisions, and recurring stylistic signatures. Treat these as hypotheses to test, not proven replacements for face time. |
| **“Authenticity”** | Authenticity should not be reduced to low production quality, mistakes or handheld footage. What matters relationally is whether behavior appears credible and consistent with the creator’s stated motives/identity. | **THEORY / adjacent DATA.** Influencer/parasocial work supports credibility and relational perceptions, but there is not strong evidence for a universal visual “authenticity signal.” | **THEORY-derived:** Script candid uncertainty where it is real; show failed attempts when causally relevant; do not manufacture imperfection merely to simulate authenticity. |
| **Humor: incongruity and resolution** | Much humor relies on setting up one interpretation, introducing an incongruity, and allowing the audience to reinterpret it. Surprise helps, but incomprehensible incongruity is not automatically funny. | **THEORY + DATA-adjacent.** Incongruity-resolution theory is a central cognitive account of humor, and television-advertising experiments have tested how incongruity, schema familiarity and surprise affect perceived humor. | **THEORY-derived:** A joke inside an explainer should have a legible setup and quick resolution. Do not require the audience to hold a complicated joke and a complicated factual argument in working memory simultaneously. |
| **Relief after tension** | The resolution of a desired uncertain outcome can create relief, and that relief can contribute to enjoyment. Emotion therefore comes not only from the tense event but from the transition out of tension. | **DATA.** Experimental work using suspenseful simulated races found evidence that relief mediates part of the relationship between suspense and enjoyment when the outcome is desirable. | **DATA-derived:** After a major test, failure scare or emotionally heavy passage, leave enough beat for the audience to *feel the release*. Immediately stacking another threat can erase the payoff you spent time building. |
| **Callbacks** | A callback can reward memory and produce recognition, but I found no strong evidence establishing callbacks as a general retention mechanism or specifying an optimal callback interval. | **THEORY / weak direct evidence.** Humor and memory theory make callbacks plausible, but the precise creator-video claim is untested in the sources meeting this review’s bar. | **THEORY-derived:** Use callbacks as optional payoff—not as a required retention device. A callback should become understandable immediately upon recognition and should not be necessary for a new viewer to follow the argument. |
| **Music tempo and arousal** | Faster tempo generally raises arousal relative to slower tempo; musical mode and other features influence mood. These effects are real but interact with listener and task. | **DATA — Husain, Thompson & Schellenberg, 2002.** Their experiments are a key source for the arousal/mood account of music effects; later reviews emphasize that the effects do not justify a simplistic “fast music = better cognition” rule. | **DATA-derived:** Let musical energy follow intended emotional energy. Raise tempo/intensity into motion, risk or anticipation; lower it for reflection or comprehension-heavy moments. Never use tempo merely to make a weak section feel artificially urgent. |
| **Music as emotional framing** | Music can alter arousal and mood before any change in factual content; it therefore changes the state in which the viewer interprets the scene. That does **not** imply every scene benefits from scoring. | **DATA / THEORY.** The arousal–mood literature supports state changes caused by musical characteristics, while findings vary by task and listener. | **DATA-derived:** Decide the intended emotion before choosing music. Ask “What should the viewer expect/feel here?” rather than adding generic “engaging” music to every minute. |
| **Silence as a tool** | Silence can be salient when it violates an established auditory pattern, but there is little direct evidence that silence itself increases creator-video retention. | **THEORY-derived inference** from prediction-violation evidence; not a video-retention experiment. | **THEORY-derived:** Use brief silence at a reveal, emotional hit or transition **because contrast matters**, not because “silence boosts retention.” If the whole video is already quiet, silence is not an interrupt. |
| **Music changes as pattern interrupts** | A musical change can reorient attention if it is perceptually meaningful and unexpected. Constant track-switching will itself become the pattern. | **THEORY-derived inference** from auditory violation and music/arousal research. | **THEORY-derived:** Change music at story-state changes—decision, setback, escalation, payoff—not on a stopwatch. |
| **Cognitive load / limited capacity** | Viewers cannot process unlimited concurrent verbal, visual and narrative information. Extra “engagement” elements can therefore reduce comprehension even when individually interesting. | **THEORY — cognitive theory of multimedia learning.** Mayer’s framework assumes separate auditory/verbal and visual/pictorial processing with limited capacity in each. | **THEORY-derived:** During a difficult idea, reduce the number of things the viewer must independently interpret. One segment should have one dominant cognitive job: understand a claim, inspect evidence, follow a procedure, or feel a consequence. |
| **Chunking / one idea per segment** | Meaningful segmentation reduces the need to keep a long chain of unresolved information active. But “one sentence = one idea” or a fixed segment duration does not follow from working-memory research. | **THEORY + DATA.** Multimedia-learning experiments support temporal coordination and segmenting related material; no universal segment length follows. | **DATA/THEORY-derived:** Cut a segment when the viewer’s mental task changes. Keep claim → evidence → implication together when separating them would force memory retrieval across a long interval. |
| **Dual coding: speech plus visuals** | Combining complementary auditory/verbal and visual/pictorial information can exploit both channels. But merely duplicating the same words in narration and on-screen text can overload a channel when other visuals also require inspection. | **DATA — Mayer.** Across three experiments summarized in the reviewed work, animation+narration outperformed animation+narration+identical on-screen text, with reported effects around 0.69–1.65 and median ≈0.84 in that experimental context. | **DATA-derived:** Put **what the viewer needs to see** on screen and **what they need explained** in narration. Use text for labels, numbers, names and structure rather than automatically duplicating every spoken sentence. Full captions may still be justified for accessibility or sound-off viewing; that is a separate design requirement. |
| **Temporal alignment** | Corresponding narration and visuals are easier to integrate when presented together rather than separated in time. | **DATA.** Mayer reports experiments in which simultaneous corresponding animation+narration beat separated presentation, with a large median effect in the summarized studies; under some short-segment conditions the difference disappeared, showing context matters. | **DATA-derived:** Say “the crack starts here” while showing the crack, not 20 seconds before or after. Move explanatory B-roll to the sentence it explains. |
| **Speech rate** | Faster delivery increases information throughput but also processing demand. No defensible creator-video literature located here establishes a universal optimal WPM. | **THEORY / evidence gap.** Limited-capacity multimedia theory predicts that rate must interact with complexity, prior knowledge, visuals and pausing opportunities. | **THEORY-derived:** Do not give the agent a universal WPM target. Allow faster delivery for simple connective material; slow down and insert processing space around novel mechanisms, names, numbers and causal chains. |
| **Engagement versus manipulation** | A hook can win the next few seconds while harming satisfaction if the eventual content fails to justify the expectation. Current YouTube ranking is explicitly not a pure watch-time objective. | **PLATFORM DATA/STATEMENT — YouTube, current 2026 documentation.** It lists watch behavior together with negative feedback and survey responses and states that the goal includes long-term satisfaction; repeated non-resonating uploads can affect channel performance over time. | **PLATFORM-derived:** Every major curiosity gap should be **paid off in the terms implied by the packaging**. Penalize scripts that fabricate stakes, indefinitely defer an answer already available, or create a stronger claim in the hook than the evidence supports. |
| **Outrage / rage-bait** | Anger is high-arousal and therefore has evidence for increased sharing, but that does not establish anger as an optimal long-term recommendation strategy. | **DATA + PLATFORM.** Anger was associated with greater transmission in Berger & Milkman; YouTube’s present objective includes long-term satisfaction and negative/survey feedback, not merely engagement volume. | **DATA-derived:** Treat justified anger as an emotional consequence of the facts, not as an input target. Do not instruct the agent to maximize outrage. A script should lose score when it intensifies blame or moral certainty beyond the evidence merely to increase emotional arousal. |

## Section B — emotion budgeting across a video

An “emotion budget” is best treated as a **trajectory of functions**, not a demand that every second be emotionally intense. High arousal is useful for orienting, suspense and sharing, but constant high arousal destroys contrast and can compete with comprehension. Curiosity requires enough stable context to know what is missing; suspense requires time for uncertainty to unfold; relief requires tension to end; difficult information requires processing capacity.

### Long-form YouTube

| Phase | Recommended emotional job | Why | Agent rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open** | **Curiosity + relevance**, usually medium-to-high activation. Optional surprise if it directly creates the question. | A specific knowledge gap gives attention a target; unexpected evidence can increase orienting. Pure context before the viewer understands the missing information asks for attention without giving it a goal. | **THEORY/DATA-derived:** Within the opening beat, make it possible to state: “The viewer now wants to know ___ because ___.” Reject vague mystery with no identifiable missing answer. |
| **Early setup** | **Trust + competence + first progress.** Reduce arousal enough to establish rules, stakes and causal context. | A gap too large for the viewer’s prior knowledge can fail; social presence can guide attention but can also consume it. | **THEORY-derived:** Give enough explanation that the viewer can predict what would count as success/failure. Provide an early micro-payoff rather than demanding faith that all value arrives at the end. |
| **Developing middle** | **Alternation:** anticipation → evidence → partial relief → renewed question. | Suspense needs changing outcome expectations, while uninterrupted stimulus escalation risks fragmentation. Tam’s results are particularly relevant here: seeking relief from boredom through constant switching can itself increase boredom. | **DATA-derived:** Each middle segment must change the state of the main question. Alternate concentrated explanation with consequence/action rather than adding unrelated “pattern interrupts.” |
| **Major setback or reversal** | **Surprise, anxiety, frustration or renewed curiosity**, but grounded in real events. | Prediction violations orient attention; suspense grows when an obstacle changes the expected path to the outcome. | **DATA-derived:** Put the reversal where it changes the causal model, not because a retention template says “something must happen at minute seven.” |
| **Pre-payoff** | **Focused suspense.** Reduce side quests and explanatory clutter. | As the outcome approaches, competing information dilutes the dominant unresolved goal; limited-capacity models predict interference from unnecessary material. | **THEORY-derived:** Close secondary loops before the primary resolution when possible. Give the viewer one thing to anticipate. |
| **Payoff** | **Resolution + relief**, potentially followed by awe, satisfaction, humor or sadness depending on the outcome. | Relief after suspense can itself contribute to enjoyment. A payoff also closes the information deprivation that powered curiosity. | **DATA-derived:** State/show the answer clearly. Do not hide the actual result inside a montage or immediately interrupt it with a CTA. |
| **Meaning / synthesis** | **Lower arousal, higher significance.** Convert “what happened?” into “why this matters.” | High emotional activation supports transmission, but comprehension benefits from lower competing load. The emotional arc literature also distinguishes trajectory from plot: the end’s affective meaning matters independently of events. | **THEORY-derived:** After the factual payoff, spend only the time necessary to reinterpret earlier events and extract the implication. |
| **End / sharing impulse** | Where appropriate, **awe, amusement, inspiration, righteous concern or identity relevance**—preferably an emotion that is a genuine consequence of the video. | High-arousal emotions have the strongest direct evidence for sharing, not necessarily for watch time. | **DATA-derived:** Ask “What would make someone send this specific video to one person?” rather than generically asking for a share. Do not manufacture outrage purely because anger is transmissible. |

The practical long-form pattern is therefore **not** “keep arousal high.” It is closer to:

**gap → orientation → progress → uncertainty → partial payoff/relief → escalation → focused suspense → payoff → meaning.**

That pattern follows the distinct functions supported by information-gap, suspense, relief and cognitive-load research rather than treating all “engagement” as one variable.

### Shorts, TikTok and Reels

Short-form compresses the same mechanisms; it does not repeal them. YouTube’s 2026 metrics distinguish a view beginning from the first frame from an **engaged view** in which the user clicks to watch or continues past the initial seconds, underscoring that the opening in a swipe/autoplay environment is a selection boundary. YouTube does not publicly turn that into a prescribed hook duration.

| Phase | Emotion | Reasoning | Agent rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First perceptual beat** | **Immediate relevance, surprise or a legible gap.** | In a feed, the viewer does not yet owe the creator context. Prediction violation can orient, but curiosity survives only if the missing information becomes understandable. | **THEORY-derived:** Start with evidence, consequence or the key question—not a greeting or meta-introduction unless the personality itself is the product. |
| **Compression/setup** | **Curiosity becomes specific.** | An unexplained weird image may orient momentarily; a defined gap gives the viewer a reason to stay after novelty disappears. | **DATA-derived:** In one or two conceptual beats, identify the “normal expectation” and the deviation. |
| **Middle** | **Progress + controlled anticipation.** | Suspense requires meaningful changes in expected outcome, not merely more sensory activity. | **THEORY-derived:** Prefer one causal line. Remove branches that would be acceptable in long-form but force several unresolved questions into a short memory window. |
| **Pattern change** | **One meaningful reorientation if needed.** | Unexpected change can reorient attention; constant changes become predictable or distracting. | **DATA-derived:** Change framing/audio/tempo when the information state changes. Do not insert zooms, captions or sound effects merely to satisfy an interval timer. |
| **Payoff** | **Clear answer + emotional consequence.** | Curiosity must eventually be extinguished by information; relief/reward is part of what makes suspense satisfying. | **DATA-derived:** Give the promised answer on screen and in speech if necessary. The viewer should not have to infer whether the promised reveal happened. |
| **Final beat** | **Aftertaste:** humor, awe, relief, surprise or meaning. | High-arousal emotions are strong candidates for sharing, but should follow rather than replace informational fulfillment. | **DATA-derived:** A final “button” is useful if it deepens the payoff. Cut once the value is delivered rather than reopening an unrelated loop solely to force replay. |

A useful distinction for the agent is therefore:

> **Attention opener ≠ retention mechanism ≠ sharing mechanism.**

**DATA:** surprise/prediction violation is good evidence for orienting; curiosity and suspense explain sustained goal-directed attention; high-arousal emotion has substantially better evidence for sharing than for completion.

That distinction prevents a common failure mode: applying the same prescription—faster cuts, more intensity, louder music—to three different problems.

## Section C — contested, weak or misleading claims

| Repeated claim | Assessment | What the evidence actually supports |
|---|---|---|
| **“Always keep three open loops.”** | **Unsupported / folklore.** | **THEORY:** information-gap theory provides no numerical loop limit; limited-capacity models argue against indefinite accumulation but do not produce “three.” |
| **“A curiosity gap must close within 30 seconds / 60 seconds / X% of the video.”** | **Unsupported.** | No credible source located establishes a universal video gap duration. Gap tolerance should instead be inferred from continuing progress, viewer value and actual retention data. |
| **“Curiosity is prediction error.”** | **Too reductive.** | Prediction violation produces surprise/orienting; curiosity involves motivation to acquire missing information. They can form a sequence but are not interchangeable constructs. |
| **“Never reveal the ending early; it kills suspense.”** | **False as a universal rule.** | Gerrig’s anomalous-suspense experiments show that known outcomes can still produce suspense. The “how” can replace the “what” as the open uncertainty. |
| **“Add a pattern interrupt every X seconds.”** | **Unsupported.** | Attention responds to meaningful violation of an established pattern; a regularly scheduled interruption rapidly becomes part of the pattern. No universal interval was found. |
| **“More cuts = more retention.”** | **Contested / context-dependent.** | Some TV experiments found higher edit rates raised arousal/attention and memory across their tested ranges, while newer VR work finds some editing fragments attention and that unedited material can be more immersive. Semantic/spatial coherence matters. |
| **“If viewers are bored, make the edit faster.”** | **Potentially backwards.** | Tam et al.’s experiments found digital switching often **increased** boredom and reduced immersion rather than curing it; one broader-age experiment did not replicate the difference, so the effect is not universal. |
| **“Reagan et al. proved there are six universal story structures.”** | **Mythologized interpretation.** | Their dataset identified six common **emotional trajectories in 1,327 Gutenberg texts**. The authors explicitly distinguish emotional arc from plot, and the study does not test YouTube retention. |
| **“Negative emotion goes viral.”** | **Incomplete.** | **DATA:** arousal matters more consistently than negativity itself. Anger/anxiety can spread; low-arousal sadness tends not to show the same advantage. Positive high-arousal emotions such as awe can also be highly transmissible. |
| **“Anger maximizes watch time.”** | **Not established.** | The strongest cited emotion studies measure sharing/virality, not YouTube AVD or completion. Current YouTube ranking also incorporates satisfaction/negative feedback. |
| **“A face on screen always retains better.”** | **Unsupported.** | **DATA:** instructor presence changes visual attention and neural synchrony; other learning work finds both benefits and distraction. That is not equivalent to a general retention advantage. |
| **“Handheld, low-production footage is inherently more authentic.”** | **Weak evidence.** | Parasocial/influencer evidence concerns credibility and relationship perceptions; no robust rule was found equating a specific production aesthetic with authenticity. |
| **“Callbacks are proven retention devices.”** | **Weak direct evidence.** | They are well-established craft devices and fit recognition/incongruity accounts of humor, but I found no strong creator-video causal evidence linking callback use to retention. |
| **“Fast music improves performance/attention.”** | **Misleading.** | Tempo can raise arousal, but the “Mozart effect” literature itself moved away from direct claims that particular music intrinsically improves cognition; mood/arousal and task fit are better explanations. |
| **“Never use silence.”** | **Unsupported.** | Auditory-change research predicts that contrast can orient attention. A sudden absence of sound can therefore be useful precisely when sound has established an expectation; no creator-retention experiment supports a blanket anti-silence rule. |
| **“Change music every 30/60 seconds.”** | **Unsupported.** | Musical change is better tied to emotional/story-state change. The evidence supports arousal effects and attention to violated auditory patterns, not a timer. |
| **“Put every spoken word on screen because dual coding says text + speech is better.”** | **Incorrect reading of multimedia theory.** | Mayer reports a **redundancy effect**: in visually demanding narrated animations, adding identical printed narration hurt learning across several experiments. Complementary channels help; redundant competition can hurt. |
| **“One idea per segment means every shot should be extremely short.”** | **Category error.** | Cognitive segmentation refers to manageable meaning units, not rapid cutting. A long shot can contain one cognitive task; ten fast cuts can contain ten competing tasks. |
| **“There is a scientifically optimal creator speech rate such as 150/180/200 WPM.”** | **Unsupported by this evidence base.** | Limited-capacity theory predicts interaction with difficulty, visuals, familiarity and listener ability. No cross-platform universal WPM optimum meeting the requested evidence bar was found. |
| **“YouTube rewards watch time, therefore anything that keeps viewers watching is good.”** | **Outdated/incomplete.** | Current YouTube documentation explicitly says the system seeks **long-term viewer satisfaction** and uses survey responses, negative feedback and positive interactions alongside watch behavior. |
| **“YouTube has a current public ‘regretted views’ ranking metric creators should optimize.”** | **Not verified as a current creator-facing metric.** | In the current 2026 recommendation documentation located for this review, YouTube describes long-term satisfaction, surveys, “Not interested,” likes, shares, comments and watch behavior. I would not hard-code “regretted views” as a named metric unless YouTube republishes it as such. |
| **“YouTube explicitly penalizes rage-bait as a named ranking category.”** | **Not established by the current first-party source located.** | YouTube documents satisfaction and viewer feedback, which give a plausible mechanism for manipulative content to underperform long term, but that is different from an explicit “rage-bait penalty.” |
| **“TikTok’s exact recommendation weights are known: completion X%, likes Y%, shares Z%.”** | **Unsupported.** | TikTok maintains an official “How TikTok recommends content” explainer, but the current live page was not retrievable here and no official fixed weight formula meeting the source bar was found. Claims giving exact weighting should therefore be rejected. |
| **“Instagram has a documented 2026 rage-bait penalty.”** | **Not established in this research.** | I did not find a current Meta first-party source cleanly substantiating that specific claim. The agent should not transform general advice about healthy engagement into an invented ranking factor. |

The Tam result is particularly important because it undermines a piece of creator folklore at the conceptual level. In several experiments, people **thought** the ability to skip/switch would make viewing less boring; experimentally, switching often made them more bored, less satisfied and less immersed. That does not prove creators should edit slowly, but it does mean “the modern viewer requires stimulation changes every few seconds” is not a safe psychological premise.

There is also a useful ethical distinction between **creating uncertainty** and **withholding fulfillment**. **THEORY:** curiosity works because missing information gains value; suspense works because a meaningful outcome remains unresolved. Neither theory requires deception. **PLATFORM:** YouTube says its target is long-term satisfaction, and that frequent content which fails to resonate can affect channel performance over time. The best agent-level rule is therefore: **maximize legitimate anticipation subject to promise fulfillment and audience trust**, not “maximize time before answer.”

For outrage specifically, the evidence creates a real optimization conflict. **DATA:** anger is highly activating and can increase transmission. **PLATFORM:** YouTube optimizes beyond transmission and watch time. The rational creator objective is therefore not the local maximum of comments/shares on one upload, but repeat satisfaction and willingness to choose the creator again. That final sentence is an inference from the two evidence bases, not a platform-disclosed formula.

## Section D — reading list

| Rank | Source | Why it is useful to a script writer |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **George Loewenstein — “The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation” (1994).** **THEORY.** | The best conceptual antidote to vague “curiosity gap” advice: it explains that the gap must become *represented in the viewer’s mind*, not merely teased by the creator. |
| **2** | **Brewer & Lichtenstein — “Stories Are to Entertain: A Structural-Affect Theory of Stories” (1982).** **THEORY.** | Gives a remarkably usable distinction among **suspense, curiosity and surprise based on information order**—directly translatable into script structure. |
| **3** | **YouTube — “YouTube’s Recommendation System” (current 2026 Help documentation).** **PLATFORM DATA/STATEMENT.** | Essential guardrail against optimizing psychology for the wrong objective: YouTube explicitly names long-term satisfaction and multiple positive/negative viewer signals. |
| **4** | **Richard Mayer — multimedia-learning research / cognitive theory of multimedia learning.** **THEORY + DATA.** | The strongest practical foundation for deciding what to say, what to show, when captions become redundant, and why “more information” can reduce understanding. |
| **5** | **Gerrig — “Suspense in the Absence of Uncertainty” (1989).** **DATA.** | Explains why spoilers do not automatically destroy engagement and why “how did it happen?” can carry a documentary even when “what happened?” is known. |
| **6** | **Berger & Milkman — “What Makes Online Content Viral?” (2012).** **DATA.** | The clearest evidence for separating **arousal** from simple positive/negative valence when scripting moments intended to be shared. |
| **7** | **Nelson-Field et al. — research on emotions that drive viral video (2013).** **DATA, video-specific.** | Extends the high-arousal result into actual video sharing, making it more directly relevant than text-only virality studies. |

| **10** | **Frede et al. — “Knowledge Gap Illustrations Spark Curiosity” (2026).** **DATA.** | Particularly valuable for hook writing because it experimentally supports a practical idea: making the *missing knowledge itself* explicit can increase curiosity and information seeking. |
