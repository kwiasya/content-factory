# Prompt 10, items 7–9: music and sound, cognitive load, ethics and limits

Researcher notes, 2026-10-12. Scope: items 7, 8, 9 of `docs/research/prompts/10-emotion-and-attention.md`.

Tags, as the CONTEXT block defines them:
- **DATA**: a measurement over a sample. For research, DATA (lab) means a controlled experiment on video, film or narrated-animation stimuli, and DATA (field) means behavioural logs or field RCTs. One video's view count is not DATA.
- **PRACTITIONER**: a stated practice with no data shown. Tier-1 platform policy statements without numbers are tagged PRACTITIONER (platform) so they stay separate from measurements.
- **THEORY**: research or a model not tested on video, such as music-only physiology, audio-only (radio), text headlines and models.

## 7. Music and sound

| # | Claim → what it implies for video | Tag | Source | Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Tempo changes **arousal** but not mood, and mode (major or minor) changes **mood** but not arousal. The test was the same Mozart piece re-rendered at four tempo × mode combinations. → Choose tempo for energy and key or mode for valence: these are two separate levers. [verified] | THEORY | Husain, Thompson, Schellenberg, *Music Perception* 20(2) — https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2002.20.2.151 | 2002 |
| 7.2 | Faster tempi and simpler rhythms raised ventilation, blood pressure and heart rate. A randomly inserted **2-minute pause** lowered heart rate, blood pressure and ventilation **below the pre-music baseline**. n = 12 musicians and 12 controls. → Silence after a fast passage is not a neutral gap: it actively lowers arousal. It works as a "breath" and as a contrast that makes the next beat hit harder. [verified] | THEORY | Bernardi, Porta, Sleight, *Heart* 92(4):445–452 — https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600 (PMC1860846) | 2006 |
| 7.3 | The "Mozart effect" disappears once arousal and mood are held constant. Music's downstream effects run through arousal and mood, not through the piece itself. → No track is magic. Judge music by the arousal and valence it produces at that point in the video. [verified] | THEORY | Thompson, Schellenberg, Husain, *Psychological Science* 12(3) — https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00345 | 2001 |
| 7.4 | Positive or negative music on **ambiguous** film clips biased viewers' interpretation of the characters, their guesses at the ending, and their memory of objects a week later, all in a mood-congruent direction, compared with no music. → Music sets the reading of neutral footage. A POV walk with no voice gets its "meaning" largely from the track, so pick the track for the reading you want. [verified] | DATA (lab, film) | Boltz, *Music Perception* 18(4) — https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2001.18.4.427 | 2001 |
| 7.5 | Music played **before or after** a neutral-faced character (n = 177) shifted how viewers read that character's emotion toward the emotion of the music. The authors call these forward and backward affective priming. → A sting or swell does not have to sit under the shot. A cue placed just before or just after it recolours it. [verified] | DATA (lab, film) | Tan, Spackman, Bezdek, *Music Perception* 25(2) — https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.25.2.135 | 2007 |
| 7.6 | When music **foreshadows** an event, mood-*incongruent* music produced better memory (an expectancy violation). When music **accompanies** an event, mood-*congruent* music did better. → Contrast before a reveal makes the reveal memorable; matching music during it supports encoding. [verified: the Memory & Cognition 19(6) abstract in the open PDF states it directly] | DATA (lab, film) | Boltz, Schulkind, Kantra, *Memory & Cognition* 19 — https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03197154 (finding taken from the Semantic Scholar TLDR; abstract elided by the publisher) | 1991 |
| 7.7 | In a survey of 83 listeners, more than 80% reported shivers, tears, laughter or a lump in the throat to music. **Shivers** came most reliably with new or unexpected harmonies, and **tears** with sequences and appoggiaturas. → Peak moments come from a violated musical expectation, not from loudness alone. [verified] | THEORY | Sloboda, *Psychology of Music* 19(2) — https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735691192002 | 1991 |
| 7.8 | Striatal dopamine is released at peak musical emotion. The **caudate** is more involved during *anticipation* and the **nucleus accumbens** during the peak itself. → A build-up has reward value of its own, so a riser before a drop or reveal is worth keeping even when the payoff is short. | THEORY | Salimpoor et al., *Nature Neuroscience* 14(2):257–262 — https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726 | 2011 |
| 7.9 | Music induces emotion through at least six mechanisms beyond appraisal: brain-stem reflex (sudden, loud or dissonant sound), evaluative conditioning, emotional contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory and musical expectancy. Studies that do not control for the mechanism give inconsistent results. → An agent rule should name the mechanism it relies on. A hard hit on a cut is a brain-stem reflex, and a nostalgic song works through episodic memory. [verified] | THEORY | Juslin & Västfjäll, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 31(5) — https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08005293 | 2008 |
| 7.10 | **Familiar** pop and rock excerpts activated limbic, paralimbic and reward regions more than unfamiliar ones. Familiarity mattered more than liking. → This is a mechanism-level reason that recognisable or trending audio may hold people, through episodic memory and expectancy. No platform evidence was found for it in this session (see Gaps). | THEORY | Pereira et al., *PLoS ONE* 6(11):e27241 — https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0027241 | 2011 |
| 7.11 | People perceive **event boundaries** at moments of perceptual prediction error, and those boundaries regulate attention and memory. → A change in music (a new section, a drop, a cut to silence) is a cheap boundary cue. Put it where the content actually changes (a new segment, a reveal) so that the boundary helps memory instead of fragmenting it. | THEORY | Zacks et al., *Psychological Bulletin* 133(2) — https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.2.273 | 2007 |
| 7.12 | In film, cuts that break **action** continuity drive event segmentation. Cuts that only change space or time have minor effects, although every edit produces a transient spike in early visual cortex. → Cuts alone do not create "new segment" perception. A change in action or topic does, and music should follow that change, not every cut. [verified] | DATA (lab, film + fMRI) | Magliano & Zacks, *Cognitive Science* 35 — https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2011.01202.x | 2011 |
| 7.13 | Adding **background music and/or sound effects** to a narrated animation (lightning formation, braking systems) *reduced* retention and transfer. The authors call this the coherence effect. → Under information-dense voice-over, music competes for the auditory channel. Duck the music hard, or drop it, during dense VO. Bring it up in the gaps. [verified: title and abstract opening confirm the design and the negative "coherence effect"; the publisher-truncated abstract does not show the retention and transfer wording] | DATA (lab, narrated animation) | Moreno & Mayer, *J. | 7.14 | Film-craft analysis argues that music which **mirrors the picture** ("you see something sad, you hear the strings") is safe and forgettable. In the demo, removing the Iron Man workshop cue leaves the scene unchanged, and narration laid over a cue **cancels its emotion**. → Music should add information the picture lacks (a counterpoint or a foreshadow), consistent with 7.5 and 7.6. Do not run VO over the emotional peak of a cue. [verified; the narrator line is "distracts us from the emotion of the scene", so "cancels" overstates it] | PRACTITIONER (analyst; 2016, possibly outdated for platforms but this is film craft) | Every Frame a Painting, "The Marvel Symphonic Universe" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7vfqkvwW2fs | 2016-09-12 |

### Negative and contested (7)
- **The "Mozart effect" is a myth that its own line of research retracted.** The effect reduces to arousal and mood (7.3, 7.1). Do not claim that a genre "boosts attention" in itself.
- **More sound is not better under information.** Background music and sound effects hurt learning from narrated visuals (7.13). The same research group found that overload comes from fast pace combined with arousing content (8.3). This conflicts with practitioner habits of "always have a bed", wall-to-wall risers and whooshes on every cut.
- **Cut-to-the-beat and music changes as pattern interrupts:** no video or attention study testing beat-synchronised cutting was found in this session. The support is indirect only (7.11, 7.12), so this remains PRACTITIONER folklore until it is sourced.
- **Platform sound claims are unverified.** These include TikTok's widely repeated "sound-on" survey figures and "trending audio boosts reach". The TikTok for Business blog URLs now redirect to an index page with no figures. Treat such claims as unsourced.
- **The silence evidence is physiological, not about attention to video** (7.2). No study was found that measures retention or recall around silence in video.

## 8. Cognitive load

| # | Claim → what it implies for video | Tag | Source | Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | Working memory holds about **4 chunks** (range 3–5), not Miller's "7 ± 2", which Cowan describes as a rough estimate and rhetorical device. → Allow at most about 3–4 new items (names, numbers, steps) live at once in a segment. Group the rest into chunks or put them on screen. [verified] | THEORY | Cowan, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(1) — https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922 | 2001 |
| 8.2 | The **Limited Capacity Model of Mediated Message Processing (LC4MP)**: viewers have limited resources for encoding, storing and retrieving information. Structural features such as cuts, and content, both draw on those resources. → Every edit "spends" capacity. Edit density and information density come out of the same budget. | THEORY | Lang, *Journal of Communication* 50(1) — https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2000.tb02833.x | 2000 |
| 8.3 | **Fast pacing** and **arousing content** each raised arousal and the resources allocated to the message. Their **combination overloaded** viewers and lowered recognition and cued recall of the specific content. → On high-arousal moments (a shock, a reveal, conflict), *slow* the edit and cut the information. On calm, low-arousal stretches, faster pacing can help. [verified] | DATA (lab, TV messages) | Lang, Bolls, Potter, Kawahara, *J. Broadcasting & Electronic Media* 43(4) — https://doi.org/10.1080/08838159909364504 | 1999 |
| 8.4 | Speech rate interacts with **information density**: "the higher the speed, the lower the information density should be." Recognition peaked at about **170 wpm for dense** content and **190 wpm for light** content. → Set the VO rate by density: about 170 wpm for fact-heavy lines and up to about 190 wpm for light connective lines. [verified] | THEORY (radio, audio-only) | Rodero, *Media Psychology* 18 — https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2014.1002942 | 2015 |
| 8.5 | Across **6.9 million** edX viewing sessions, the median engagement time for videos **under 6 minutes** was close to 100%, and engagement fell for longer videos. **Engagement rose with speaking rate.** Brame's table gives a recommended range of 185–254 wpm, which is the review's own suggestion, not a tested optimum. → Faster delivery is not penalised and is probably preferred. Treat the 6-minute figure as a lecture-segment rule, not a platform length rule. [verified] | DATA (field logs, MOOC lectures; old) | Guo, Kim, Rubin, L@S '14 — https://doi.org/10.1145/2556325.2566239 ; summarised in Brame, *CBE—Life Sciences Education* 15(4) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/ | 2014; 2016 |
| 8.6 | Playing lecture videos at **1.5× or 2×** cost little immediate or one-week comprehension, and performance **dropped beyond 2×**. The authors caveat that this may differ by speech rate, complexity and audiovisual overlap. → Viewers can absorb faster speech than people assume. The ceiling is the density of the content, not the rate itself. [verified] | DATA (lab, lecture video) | Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn, Castel, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* — https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3899 | 2021 |
| 8.7 | **Redundancy:** adding on-screen text that duplicated *or* summarised the narration of an animation lowered retention and transfer. The text split visual attention. In the same paper, **seductive details** (interesting but irrelevant narration or video clips) lowered transfer. → Do not put full VO sentences on screen over busy footage. Every B-roll or "fun fact" insert must serve the point of its segment. [verified] | DATA (lab, narrated animation) | Mayer, Heiser, Lonn, *J. Educational Psychology* 93(1) — https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.1.187 | 2001 |
| 8.8 | **Revised redundancy principle:** **2–3 printed words** identical to the narration, placed next to the relevant part of the picture, *improved* retention (d = 0.47 and 0.70) but not transfer. The keywords guide attention. → On-screen text should be a keyword or short phrase that labels what the voice is saying, not a transcript. [verified] | DATA (lab, narrated slides) | Mayer & Johnson, *J. Educational Psychology* 100(2) — https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380 | 2008 |
| 8.9 | Meta-analysis of **57 studies**: spoken+written presentation **beat spoken-only** for learners with low prior knowledge, for **system-paced** material and for **picture-free** material. **Key terms** pulled from the narration beat verbatim text and accounted for much of the advantage. → Social video is system-paced and its audience is mostly new to the topic, which argues *for* text. A talking head with little imagery gains the most from captions. Over dense B-roll, use keywords only. [verified] | DATA (meta-analysis; mostly lab, not platform video) | Adesope & Nesbit, *J. Educational Psychology* 104(1) — https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147 | 2012 |
| 8.10 | More than 100 empirical studies show that **same-language captions** improve comprehension of, attention to and memory for video, especially for non-native viewers, learners and D/deaf viewers. → Burned-in captions are justified for an international or sound-off audience. This is the main counterweight to 8.7. [verified] | DATA (review of 100+ studies) | Gernsbacher, *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 2(1) — https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215602130 | 2015 |
| 8.11 | **Segmenting, signalling and weeding** an educational video together improved transfer and structural knowledge and lowered perceived difficulty. → Write one idea per segment with a visible boundary (a title card, a music change or a "second thing"), cue the key element, and cut what does not serve it. [verified] | DATA (lab, educational video) | Ibrahim, Antonenko, Greenwood, Wheeler, *Learning, Media and Technology* 37 — https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2011.585993 | 2011 (online) |
| 8.12 | Learner-paced **segmenting** of a narrated animation improved transfer, consistent with cognitive load theory. → Platform video cannot be paced by the viewer, so build the pauses in: a beat of breathing room (silence or a held shot) after each dense idea. [verified] | DATA (lab) | Mayer & Chandler, *J. Educational Psychology* 93(2) — https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.390 | 2001 |
| 8.13 | **Modality:** narration with visuals beats printed text with visuals because it uses both channels. The **personalisation** principle says conversational wording improves learning. → Explain in VO and show in picture. Do not make viewers read long text while they watch. Write in the first and second person. [verified] | THEORY (review summarising Mayer & Moreno 2003 and Mayer 2008) | Brame 2016 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/ | 2016 |
| 8.14 | **Expertise reversal:** techniques that cut load for novices can lose their benefit or **hurt** experienced learners. → Captions, recaps and signposting that help a general audience can bore a niche, expert audience. Adjust density to the audience, not to a fixed rule. [verified] | THEORY (review) | Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler, Sweller, *Educational Psychologist* 38(1) — https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4 | 2003 |

**Example (8.x).** No platform video with a published, timestamped load or density analysis was found in this session (see Gaps).

### Negative and contested (8)
- **Redundancy versus captions.** 8.7 (on-screen text hurts) conflicts with 8.9 and 8.10 (text helps). Moderators: whether pictures are present (text plus busy pictures hurts), pacing (system-paced favours text), prior knowledge, language, and whether the text is keywords or verbatim. The agent rule should be conditional.
- **"7 ± 2" is outdated.** Cowan's estimate of about 4 replaces it (8.1).
- **"Keep it under 6 minutes"** comes from MOOC lectures measured by fraction watched. It is not a YouTube or short-form finding and should not be used as a platform length rule.
- **Seductive details versus retention practice.** Lab work says interesting-but-irrelevant inserts hurt *learning* (8.7). Practitioners use them to hold *attention*. Learning is not watch time, so no direct test of this conflict was found.
- **Faster is not always better.** Comprehension holds to about 2× (8.6), but Lang (8.3) and Rodero (8.4) show that the cost appears when rate or pace is combined with high density or high arousal.
- Two meta-analyses exist but their numbers were not retrieved. Schneider et al. 2018 on signalling (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001) and Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 on seductive details (https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4) both report effects in the directions above. Their abstracts are elided by the publishers, so no effect sizes are quoted here.

### Negative and contested (9)
- **No damage to trust from clickbait in one test.** Munger et al. randomly assigned people to clickbait or traditional headlines in two experiments. They found **no effect** on affective polarisation, information retention or trust in media. This conflicts with 9.21. Both are one-shot news experiments, and neither measures repeated exposure to a single creator. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfaa008 (*Public Opinion Quarterly*, 2020). THEORY. [verified] [tag → THEORY: headline experiment, not tested on video]
- **The YouTube "rabbit hole" is contested.** In a panel of more than 300,000 Americans (2016–2019), the authors found no evidence that YouTube recommendations systematically cause far-right consumption. Consumption reflected individual preferences across the web. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2101967118 (Hosseinmardi et al., *PNAS* 2021). DATA. [verified] Claims that "the algorithm rewards outrage" should be scoped to the platform and the period.
- **Satisfaction surveys are not a moral filter.** YouTube's own engineer says people rate fringe content as satisfying (9.8). Valued watch time protects against *disappointment*, not against harm.
- **The engagement-versus-satisfaction gap is mainly political.** In Milli et al. (9.17), users slightly *preferred* the algorithm's tweets overall, and the dissatisfaction centred on political and out-group content. Do not generalise this to all high-arousal content.
- **The negativity evidence comes from text, not video.** 9.13–9.15 measure headlines and posts. No study was found that measures negativity in titles or thumbnails against later watch time or satisfaction on YouTube.
- **Platform policies are mostly unmeasured.** Apart from YouTube's 70% borderline figure (9.2), the platforms state demotions without showing their effect on creators.

5. **Cognitive-load evidence comes from education.** Almost all of 8.x uses learning outcomes (retention and transfer), not watch time or swipe-away. Guo 2014 is the only behavioural engagement dataset, and it is MOOC data from 2014.
6. **Short-form captions and on-screen text.** The widely quoted "X% watch sound-off" and "captions add Y% view time" figures (Verizon/Publicis 2019; Facebook 2016) were not retrieved and would be outdated anyway.
