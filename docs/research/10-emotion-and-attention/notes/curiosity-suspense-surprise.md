# 10 · Notes: items 1–3 (curiosity, suspense, surprise / pattern interrupts)

Researcher share: items 1, 2, 3 of `docs/research/prompts/10-emotion-and-attention.md`. Compiled 2026-10-12.

**Method.** 17 web searches, then the shared web-search budget for this run ran out. After that I used only direct fetches: the primary papers (PDFs extracted with `pdftotext`), abstracts looked up by DOI through the OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar APIs, and YouTube metadata plus auto-captions pulled with `yt-dlp`. **Timestamps** are the start times of YouTube's auto-generated English caption cues (`en-orig`), downloaded 2026-10-12, so expect ±1–2 s. Durations come from `yt-dlp`.

**Tags** (CONTEXT block definitions):
- **DATA-field**: measured on a real platform or a field sample, such as an A/B test, retention data or a large log dataset.
- **DATA-lab**: a controlled experiment that used actual video or TV stimuli. It counts as DATA because it was tested on video, but it is lab-scale.
- **PRACTITIONER**: a creator's or analyst's stated practice, with no data shown.
- **THEORY**: research or a model that was not tested on video, such as trivia, text or headline studies.
- **PLATFORM**: a tier-1 statement of a platform rule or mechanism. It is evidence of how the system scores content, not of what viewers feel.

## 1. Curiosity

### 1a. Mechanism: how a gap opens and closes

| # | Claim | Tag | Source (date) | What it predicts for video / rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Curiosity is a form of "cognitively induced deprivation" that arises when attention focuses on a perceived gap in knowledge. Its typical traits: it is intense, transient and impulsive, and it **tends to disappoint when satisfied**. | THEORY [verified] | Loewenstein, *Psych. Bulletin* 116(1) [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) (1994) | A gap only works if the viewer *notices* it, so say the question out loud. Because resolution tends to disappoint, the payoff has to exceed the promise. |
| 1.2 | Curiosity is an **inverted-U function of confidence**. With confidence P, the fitted curve was curiosity = 0.49 − 0.39P + 4.77P(1−P). It is highest when the viewer half-knows the answer (fMRI study, n = 19, 40 trivia questions). | THEORY [verified] | Kang et al., *Psych. Science* 20(8) [PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf) (2009) | Open gaps on topics the viewer already partly knows. A question about something wholly unfamiliar ("what is X?") or wholly obvious generates little pull. |
| 1.3 | Curiosity predicts willingness to **pay a cost to close the gap**. Participants spent scarce tokens (n = 10) or waited a random 5–25 s for the answer (n = 20) more often at high curiosity. Curiosity ratings correlated with caudate activity, a region linked to anticipated reward. | THEORY [verified] | Kang et al. 2009 (as above) | Curiosity is treated as a reward signal, and "waiting" is its behavioural measure. On a platform, the equivalent of waiting is not swiping away. |
| 1.4 | Curiosity improves memory for the answer, most of all for **surprising answers** (the participant had guessed wrong). Recall 11–16 days later, high vs low curiosity: d = 1.11. Only 12 of 16 participants returned. | THEORY [verified] | Kang et al. 2009, Exp. 2 | Curiosity followed by a prediction error makes a strong combination: ask the viewer to guess, then show that the guess was wrong. Sample very small. |
| 1.5 | During high-curiosity states, memory improved even for **unrelated material** shown in the anticipation window (faces shown during a 14-s wait before the answer). The effect held on the immediate test and, for confidently recognised faces only, on the 1-day test. The face-memory benefit tracked anticipatory midbrain (SN/VTA) and hippocampal activity; nucleus accumbens activity rose with curiosity but did not track the face-memory benefit. n = 19; the authors call the effect "small, but significant". | THEORY [verified; corrected from the paper: the 1-day effect held only for confident recognition, and the face-memory benefit tracked SN/VTA and hippocampus, not nucleus accumbens] | Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, *Neuron* 84(2) [PMC4252494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/) (2014) | Put the context the viewer needs (setup, exposition) *inside* an open loop rather than before the loop opens. |
| 1.6 | Learning is driven by an **information prediction error** (the gap between the expected and the received information) more than by the absolute value of the information. Positive information raised curiosity and long-term memory more than negative. | THEORY [verified] | Marvin & Shohamy, *JEP: General* 145(3) [doi:10.1037/xge0000140](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000140), [lab PDF](https://shohamylab.zuckermaninstitute.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2017-02/Curiosity.pdf) (2016) | A payoff lands when it is *better or different* than expected. A predictable payoff closes the gap without reward. |
| 1.7 | Whether curiosity peaks at *intermediate* or at *maximal* uncertainty depends on the environment. When a stimulus is likely to come up again, intermediate uncertainty wins (the information-gap pattern). Otherwise, novelty wins. | THEORY [verified] | Dubey & Griffiths, *Psych. Review* [doi:10.1037/rev0000175](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000175) (2019 online / 2020) | The "half-known" rule is conditional. Pure novelty, such as an unseen place on a POV walk, can also pull. |
| 1.8 | **Generating a prediction** increases curiosity about the answer. | THEORY [verified] | Brod & Breitwieser, *npj Sci. Learn.* [PMC6803639](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6803639/) (2019) | Explicit "guess before I show you" prompts are backed by theory. Not tested on video. |
| 1.9 | **Teasing effect** (7 studies): creating and then resolving an uncertainty gives a better hedonic experience than receiving the same information all at once. People nonetheless *choose* the all-at-once version. Applied to an ad, a create-and-resolve structure improved attitude toward the product and willingness to try it. | THEORY (incl. ad stimuli) [verified from abstract] | Ruan, Hsee & Lu, *JMR* 55(4) [doi:10.1509/jmr.15.0346](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.15.0346) (2018) | A writer's intuition to "just say it" is unreliable, because people prefer all-at-once when asked yet enjoy the teased version more. Short tease-then-resolve beats are justified even in very short videos. |
| 1.10 | **Curiosity in a watching task** (Evolving Line Drawing: a drawing reveals itself gradually; >2,000 people aged 19–76). Curiosity evolves *within* one episode. Higher curiosity made people **keep watching instead of skipping to the spoiler**. | DATA-lab [verified: PNAS abstract confirms the spoiler-choice result; >2,000 and ages 19–76 from the SciAm piece] | Hsiung, Poh, Huettel & Adcock, *PNAS* 120(43) [doi:10.1073/pnas.2301974120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2301974120) (2023); authors' summary in [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/no-spoilers-please-why-curiosity-makes-us-patient/) (2024-04-09) | This is the closest lab analogue to "swipe or stay". Curiosity drives staying through the process, not only toward the answer. |
| 1.11 | Curiosity **peaks twice**: once at maximum uncertainty and again just before the reveal. Its emotional tone shifts from playful joy at the first peak to **frustration** near the reveal. | DATA-lab [verified, hedged: the twin peaks are confirmed; SciAm says the tone "may shift" toward urgent discomfort, and the PNAS abstract says curiosity can be positive or negative depending on the stage, so treat the frustration part as tentative] | Hsiung et al. 2023, per the authors' SciAm summary (2024) | This is the only sourced signal on "tolerance". Near resolution, curiosity turns aversive, so a stalled reveal at that point risks exits. Pay off soon after the pre-reveal peak. |

## 2. Suspense and anticipation

| # | Claim | Tag | Source (date) | What it predicts for video / rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | **Structural-affect theory.** Stories are built to evoke one of three affective patterns, depending on the order in which events are told. Suspense comes from chronological order with the initiating event early and the outcome withheld. Surprise comes from withholding crucial information and revealing it unexpectedly. Curiosity comes from omitting a significant early event from the telling while letting the reader know it is missing, then giving enough later information to reconstruct it (the classic mystery; often glossed as "outcome first, events after"). | THEORY [verified against the 1982 tech report; curiosity wording corrected to the report's definition] | Brewer & Lichtenstein, *J. Pragmatics* 6 [doi:10.1016/0378-2166(82)90021-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-2166(82)90021-2); [tech report](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/17648) (1982) | The script agent can choose the emotion by choosing the *order* of events: a cold open that shows the result produces curiosity, while a chronological countdown produces suspense. |
| 2.2 | **Affective disposition (Zillmann).** Suspense is anxious concern for a *liked* protagonist facing an uncertain outcome. The theory predicts more suspense when a bad outcome is likely. | THEORY [verified as a second-hand summary: Kaspar et al. 2016 states the bad-outcome prediction (H1b) and quotes Zillmann] | Zillmann, as summarised in [Kaspar et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5156838/) and [Chun et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.558234/full) (I did not read the primary chapters) | Without a person to root for, there is little suspense. On a faceless POV walk, the "protagonist" has to be the viewer's own stake. |
| 2.3 | **Suspense in news (non-fiction).** Three experiments (n = 263, 255, 599). *Perceived* liking of the protagonist (all three studies) and *perceived* likelihood of a **good** ending (Studies 2 and 3, not Study 1) correlated positively with suspense. Suspense correlated with appreciation (r ≈ .32–.50 in Studies 1–2) and lingering interest (r ≈ .47–.58 in Studies 1–2). *Manipulated* liking and outcome had weak or inconsistent effects. This contradicts the disposition-theory prediction that a likely bad outcome raises suspense. | THEORY (text news) [verified; ranges and per-study detail corrected] | Kaspar, Zimmermann & Wilbers, *Front. Psychol.* 7:1913 [PMC5156838](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5156838/) (2016) | Suspense works in non-fiction. Hope for a good outcome may beat dread. The audience's reading of the protagonist matters more than the writer's intent, so test it. |
| 2.4 | **Anomalous suspense.** Readers of suspenseful versions of stories about *known* historical events were slower to verify the true outcome. Absorption temporarily displaces knowledge of the ending. | THEORY [verified from the abstract: J. Memory & Language 28(6) 1989; stimuli were short texts about known outcomes] | Gerrig, *J. Memory & Language* (1989), [Stony Brook record](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/suspense-in-the-absence-of-uncertainty/) | A known outcome, such as "the building did not fall", can still grip if the telling puts the viewer inside the moment of uncertainty. |
| 2.5 | Repeated exposure to the same suspense film segment (the *Run Lola Run* chase, each time followed by a different ending; n = 49). Suspense fell from 6.80 to 5.08 to 4.69 over three viewings and arousal from 7.16 to 6.65 to 6.08, while **enjoyment did not change**. Suspense was reduced but not eliminated. | DATA-lab (film) [verified; stimulus description corrected] | Chun, Park & Shi, *Front. Psychol.* 11 [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.558234](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.558234/full) (2020) | Rewatch or known-outcome suspense is real, but about a quarter to a third weaker. |
| 2.6 | **Suspense and surprise defined formally.** Suspense in a period is the variance of next period's beliefs. Surprise is the size of the belief change from the last period. The paper derives the information-release schedule that maximises each, with applications to mysteries, game shows and sports. | THEORY [verified from abstract] | Ely, Frankel & Kamenica, *JPE* 123(1) [doi:10.1086/677350](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/doi10.1086-677350.html) (2015) | A reveal plan should keep the *next* step's outcome uncertain (suspense) and occasionally move belief sharply (surprise, a twist). Only the abstract was read. |
| 2.7 | **Domain-general tension model.** Conflict, instability, dissonance or uncertainty trigger predictive processes aimed at future events that carry emotional weight. The same mechanism applies to music, film and narrative. | THEORY [verified from abstract] | Lehne & Koelsch, *Front. Psychol.* 6:79 [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00079](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00079) (2015) | This links suspense in a script to tension in the music (item 7). Both are prediction under uncertainty. |
| 2.8 | **Reveal pacing.** Curiosity peaks at maximum uncertainty and again just before resolution, turning frustrated near the reveal (1.11). Teased resolution beats all-at-once (1.9). | DATA-lab / THEORY [verified] | Hsiung et al. 2023; Ruan et al. 2018 | Reveal in steps (partial, then full). Do not stall at the final step. |
| 2.9 | The MrBeast doc describes re-engagement as a twist or escalation, for example a *new rule* in a challenge, every ~3 min. | PRACTITIONER [verified: OMR (2024-09-19) gives the new-rule example and the second re-engagement after 3 more minutes] | [OMR (DE)](https://omr.com/de/daily/mrbeast-doc-pdf-leak) and [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production) (2024-09) | Raise uncertainty periodically by changing the rules (see the example below). |

### 2. Negative and contested (suspense)

| # | Finding | Tag | Source (date) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.N1 | **Cliffhangers raised arousal (self-report and electrodermal activity) but did not raise enjoyment or intention to continue watching.** Lab study, N = 133, 3–4 episodes of a drama ending with or without a cliffhanger. ## Gaps

1. **Search budget.** The shared web-search limit ran out after 17 queries. These were not reached:
   - Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012, *JMR* "Emotion-induced engagement in internet video advertisements" (surprise and joy vs zapping). Its DOI lookup failed.
   - Knobloch et al. 2004 (discourse structure in news).
   - Hoeken & van Vliet 2000.
   - Comisky & Bryant 1982 (suspense peaks at high but not total threat).
   - Bezdek et al. 2015 (suspense narrows attention).
   - Zillmann's primary chapters.
   - Paddy Galloway, 1of10 and Film Booth breakdowns on open loops and re-hooks.
   - TikTok or Meta creative guidance on pacing.
   Disposition theory (2.2) is cited only second-hand.
2. **How many gaps can stay open?** No study found measures this for video or narrative. Theory offers only working-memory limits (item 8, another researcher). The examples show one master gap plus one active sub-gap at a time, but that is observation, not evidence.
3. **How long will viewers tolerate an unclosed gap?** Lab waits are seconds long (5–25 s). The only signal for longer spans is the curiosity-to-frustration shift near the reveal (Hsiung 2023). The real videos above hold gaps for 18–24 min with periodic reminders. No retention-graph evidence links gap length to drop-off.
4. **Pattern interrupts against retention on YouTube, TikTok or Reels.** No public platform data or creator retention graph was found that isolates cut rate or interrupt frequency. All edit-density evidence comes from 1990–2010 lab TV studies or ad-zapping studies.
5. **Conflicts.**
   - Spoilers: Leavitt & Christenfeld 2011 vs Johnson & Rosenbaum 2015.
   - Pacing vs fantasy as the cause of executive-function drops: Lillard 2011 vs 2015.
   - Curiosity's memory benefit: Kang 2009 and Gruber 2014 (n = 12–19) vs Wade & Kidd 2019 (small boost).
   - Edits: they raise memory within a scene (Lang 2000) but lower memory when content changes (Lang 1993) or when combined with arousing content (Lang 1999).
6. **Not fully verified.**
   - Banerjee & Urminsky: I read only the January 2021 preliminary draft. The published 2025 coefficients may differ.
   - The MrBeast doc is second-hand, and its authenticity is unconfirmed.
   - Gruber 2014 effect sizes come from secondary summaries.
   - Hsiung 2023: the sample size (>2,000) and the twin curiosity peaks come from the authors' SciAm piece, not the paper's methods.
7. **Recency.** Platform and creator evidence from 2024–2026: YouTube A/B by watch time, YouTube's clickbait enforcement (2024-12), the MrBeast doc (2024), and the six example videos (2024–2026). The Veritasium clickbait video (2021), Guo 2014 and the Upworthy data (2013–15) are older and flagged.
